# Economic History draft review

Reviewed October 13, 2026. Draft: 60 original constructed questions, six units of ten, plus an aligned outline. No activation, runtime edits or rendered-site check.

## Verified sources, scope and prerequisites

Verified live official course materials from two R1 institutions:

- [MIT 14.731 Economic History, Fall 2006 syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-731-economic-history-fall-2006/pages/syllabus/). The graduate calendar covers agrarian economies, Malthusian demography, demographic transition, financial institutions, industrialization across regions, slavery and its aftermath, labor participation, corporations, depression and recovery. Its stated goals combine historical processes with applied economic reasoning and critical examination of research.
- [UC Berkeley Economics 113, American Economic History, Spring 2015 syllabus](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~olney/spring15/econ113/syllabus.pdf). This upper-division course lists introductory micro and macro as its official prerequisite and emphasizes economic explanations, statistics and interpreting econometric evidence. Its schedule includes transport and industrialization, fertility, immigration, labor, banking and finance, slavery, railroads, the Great Depression and financial crises.

Source URLs document curricular coverage, not an assertion that these syllabi establish every disputed historical proposition. The draft does not claim actual numerical effects for named historical events. Institutional rules, populations, accounts, contracts, coefficients and dates inside exercises are constructed. Mechanisms are conditional on explicit assumptions; observed records are not treated as proof of a unique causal account. This keeps financial history and colonial/coercive institutions substantive without inventing consensus about their historical effects.

Prerequisites in the outline: introductory microeconomics and macroeconomics, algebra, ratios and percentages. Elementary statistics and causal-inference concepts support archival and identification items. The curriculum plan combines economic history and history of thought; this bounded course follows the requested economic-history scope. It includes the distinction between explanation and normative endorsement, but does not purport to replace a separate history-of-thought survey.
