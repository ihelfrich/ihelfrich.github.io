Katia Antunes has her ticket. Lisbon on June fourth, Dublin on June twenty-second, back to Atlanta the first week of July. She's been my tutoring client for the better part of a year, and the trip is the start of her own research career.

The premise of the trip is straightforward. I completed a descriptive, noncausal decomposition on the U.S. New Markets Tax Credit (the [CDE decomposition I wrote about](/writing/cde-decomposition-nmtc)). In the base decomposition, observed CDE composition contributes −0.185, or 86 percent of the explained movement; the mechanism remains unresolved.

The question Katia is going to Lisbon to answer is whether the same logic transfers to the European setting. Whether the Portuguese Sociedades de Garantia Mútua (the four mutual-guarantee societies that intermediate most national-level SME credit guarantees) and the Irish Strategic Banking Corporation of Ireland (which works through commercial-bank on-lenders) show the same decomposition pattern. Whether the apparent rural-vs-urban gap in EU blended finance is, like the U.S. version, mostly a between-intermediary phenomenon.

I genuinely don't know what she's going to find.

## Why I don't know

The U.S. result depends on a specific feature of the U.S. setting. The CDFI Fund picks which Community Development Entities get NMTC allocation authority in each round. The Fund's allocation process is partly competitive (a written application that gets scored) and partly geographic (a stated preference for rural-targeted CDEs in certain rounds). The result is that the population of CDEs deploying rural capital is institutionally distinct from the population deploying urban capital, and the two populations differ on more dimensions than just where they deploy.

That selection mechanism is specific to how the CDFI Fund operates. It is not the only way to organize an intermediated blended-finance program. The Portuguese SGM structure organizes intermediaries differently: the four SGMs (Lisgarante for the south, Norgarante for the north, Garval for the center, Agrogarante specialized in agriculture) each have geographic or sectoral mandates that overlap less than the U.S. CDE mandates. The "rural" deployment is concentrated in one specific intermediary (Agrogarante) by institutional design. The Irish SBCI structure is different again: SBCI itself doesn't deploy, it funds commercial banks (AIB, Bank of Ireland, PTSB, plus several specialist lenders) at subsidized rates, and the commercial banks deploy on their own balance sheets with SBCI as a guarantor. The "rural" deployment in Ireland is distributed across the commercial banks rather than concentrated in a single intermediary.

Three institutional designs, three different shapes of intermediation. The U.S. result might transfer to all of them; it might transfer to one; it might transfer to none. The honest version of the answer depends on which dimension of the U.S. decomposition is doing the work, and that's not something I can figure out from a U.S. dataset alone.

## What the answer could look like

Scenario one: the decomposition replicates. Across Portugal, Ireland, and the U.S., the apparent rural-urban gap in leverage or in deployment volume is mostly between-intermediary selection. The within-intermediary penalty is small or zero. The "rural credit problem" is institutionally generic. The policy lesson generalizes: the cheaper fix in any pass-through blended-finance program is to route more rural-targeted capital through high-leverage urban-experienced intermediaries with rural carve-outs, rather than to subsidize rural projects more. That's a publishable cross-country comparative paper with significant policy reach.

Scenario two: the decomposition replicates partially. Maybe it shows up in Portugal (where the SGMs have specialized mandates similar to U.S. CDEs) but not in Ireland (where SBCI uses a different intermediary geometry). That's a more interesting paper, because it tells us which feature of the U.S. setting matters. If the institutional analogy with Portuguese SGMs reproduces the U.S. result and the institutional difference with the Irish SBCI doesn't, then we know the mechanism is "intermediary selection by mandate," not "intermediary heterogeneity in general."

Scenario three: the decomposition doesn't replicate in either European setting. The within-intermediary rural penalty is real and large in Portuguese and Irish data. That tells us the U.S. result is partly an artifact of the CDFI Fund's specific allocation rules and not a generalizable phenomenon. That's also publishable. The paper becomes a careful comparative study with an unusual conclusion: the U.S. mechanism doesn't transfer, and here's why. The policy lesson narrows but doesn't disappear: in U.S. settings the intermediary-selection lever is cheap; in EU settings something else is going on and we need to figure out what.

I would publish in any of the three scenarios. The trip is valuable in all three.

## The thing I want to emphasize

This is what I think real applied research feels like when you're doing it honestly. You have a result in one setting. You think the mechanism is general. You have specific reasons to think the mechanism is general and specific reasons to worry it's setting-specific. You do not know which one is right. You go look.

The kind of paper that emerges from this trip will be very different in each scenario. The scenario-one paper would be a clean policy paper with a strong cross-country claim. The scenario-two paper would be a mechanism paper with a sharper analytical contribution. The scenario-three paper would be a setting-specificity paper that draws institutional limits around the U.S. result. I don't have a strong prior on which one I'm writing. I have weak priors. The most likely outcome is some version of scenario two, because the U.S. and Portuguese institutional structures are similar enough that I'd guess the mechanism transfers in some form, while the U.S.-Irish differences are large enough that I'd guess the transfer is partial.

But weak priors mean I could easily be wrong. The trip is exactly the kind of empirical work that disciplines weak priors. You go, you do the interviews, you get the disbursement data, you run the decomposition, and the data tell you which scenario you're in. The honest researcher updates and writes the paper for that scenario, not for the scenario they were hoping for.

## What we're doing this week

Katia and I built a stakeholder map in the spring. AD&C in Lisbon (the national managing authority for EU funds in Portugal). Banco Português de Fomento (the Portuguese promotional bank). GPP in the agriculture ministry (which has run impact evaluations on Portuguese rural credit). Banco de Portugal Economics Research (which has the credit register). The four SGMs themselves. In Dublin: SBCI, ISIF, DRCD, ESRI, Teagasc, UCD Geary. Twenty-five interviews scheduled.

The interview goal isn't to test the hypothesis. The interview goal is to figure out what data exist and how to get them. The hypothesis-testing happens in the regression once we have the data home.

That's the realistic timeline. The trip is the data-access trip. The decomposition runs in the fall. The cross-country paper comes out, with the appropriate caveats and the appropriate humility, in early 2027.

## What I'm telling Katia

We've talked about this several times. The thing I want her to take away from the trip is that you go in willing to be wrong. The U.S. result is clean. The transfer to Europe is not guaranteed. If the European data say the U.S. result doesn't transfer, that's a finding, not a failure. Write the paper that finding deserves.

I think she'll do this well. The combination of her policy-side training, her language skills (she speaks Portuguese, which matters more than I think most people realize for the depth of interview access in Lisbon), and her willingness to push back on the U.S. framing makes her exactly the right person for the trip. The proposed European follow-on is separate from the solo-authored U.S. NMTC manuscript.

Whatever the answer is, we'll write it.
