Chapter 5

Index numbers and circularity

Web edition. Figure 5.1 is live: its prices and quantities are the chapter’s, and you can change them.

Here is a small invented example, and the whole chapter grows out of it. Four countries, A to D, and three goods: grain, fuel and housing. Table 5.1 gives each country’s prices, in one common currency unit, and the quantities its households buy. Nothing about the numbers is meant to be realistic; they are chosen so that the countries eat quite differently, which is what makes price comparisons hard.

A B C D A B C D
grain 1.0 0.6 1.5 2.2 10 14 6 3
fuel 1.0 1.8 0.7 1.4 4 2 8 5
housing 1.0 1.1 2.0 0.8 6 5 3 12

Ask how much dearer it is to live in C than in A. One standard answer, the Fisher index that the next section defines, is 1.344: C is about 34 percent dearer. Now ask a more roundabout question. How much dearer is B than A, and how much dearer is C than B? The same index says 0.908 and 1.338. Chain them and A to B to C gives 0.908×1.338=1.215, which says C is 21 percent dearer than A, not 34. Three countries, one index formula, two answers to the same question, and no reason to prefer either.

That is the circularity problem. It has a fix that statistical offices use every day, and the fix turns out to be a piece of geometry familiar from least squares: a least-squares projection on a graph. This chapter builds the problem, then the fix, then draws the picture. At the end I say what the research cycle found when it tried the fix on simulated economies, including a mistake of ours that a closer reading corrected.

5.1Two countries at a time

A bilateral price index answers one question about one pair of countries j and k: what does the same living cost in k relative to j? Write pij and qij for the price and quantity of good i in country j, and sij=pijqij/∑ipijqij for its budget share. Two classical answers fix a basket and price it in both places: Ljk=∑ipikqij∑ipijqij,Pjk=∑ipikqik∑ipijqik.Ljk=∑ipikqij∑ipijqij,Pjk=∑ipikqik∑ipijqik. The Laspeyres index Ljk prices j’s basket at k’s prices. The Paasche index Pjk prices k’s basket. Each is a perfectly good answer to a slightly different question, and when two countries’ baskets differ the questions differ a lot. For A and B in the example, LAB=0.990 and PAB=0.833; for B and C they are 1.851 and 0.967. When each country buys relatively more of what is cheap there, the Paasche comes out below the Laspeyres, and in this table it does so for every pair.

An index that depends on the choice of basket has an obvious repair, and it is the one in common use: average the two. The Fisher index is the geometric mean, Fjk=LjkPjk, and the Törnqvist index averages the log price changes with the two countries’ budget shares as weights, log⁡Tjk=∑i12(sij+sik)(log⁡pik−log⁡pij). Table 5.2 computes all four for every pair. Both Fisher and Törnqvist have the property I need and Laspeyres alone lacks: the index from k to j is the reciprocal of the index from j to k. For Laspeyres the reverse index is Lkj=1/Pjk, a different number. In logs, writing tjk=log⁡Fjk, the symmetric indexes satisfy tkj=−tjk, so a whole set of bilateral comparisons is an antisymmetric array, one number per pair.

Notice what each number in the table used. The A-to-B Fisher index used A’s and B’s baskets and nobody else’s. The B-to-C index used B’s and C’s. The A-to-C index used A’s and C’s. Three comparisons of the same three price vectors, each weighted by a different pair of baskets. Hold that thought; it is the whole reason for what comes next.

5.2Circularity

Suppose the three comparisons did come from three price levels, one per country, PA,PB,PC, and each index were just a ratio of two of them: Ijk=Pk/Pj. Then going from A to B to C must agree with going from A to C directly, since (PB/PA)(PC/PB)=PC/PA. A set of indexes that passes this is called transitive, or is said to pass Fisher’s circularity test, and in logs it is a statement about loops: the sum of t around any closed route is zero, gABC=tAB+tBC+tCA=0. Conversely, if every triangle closes, fix a base country and define each country’s level as its index from the base. Every other index is then a ratio of levels. So “passes circularity on every triangle” and “comes from a set of price levels” are one and the same statement, provided every pair of countries has an index.

The Fisher indexes of the example do not close. Figure 5.1 walks around the triangle ABC, adding up log indexes as it goes. The walk starts at zero, drops by 0.096 to reach B, climbs by 0.291 to reach C, drops by 0.295 on the way home to A, and arrives at −0.100 instead of zero. The gap is gABC=−0.100, which is the product 0.905 in levels. The other three triangles give gABD=−0.046, gACD=−0.049 and gBCD=−0.103.

We did not choose these numbers to make the gap large; we chose them so the baskets differ, and the gap followed. What makes the gap appear is the observation at the end of the last section. Each edge averages the same goods with a different set of weights. If every edge used one common set of weights, say the world’s average basket, the index would be a weighted average of log prices with fixed weights, and fixed-weight averages are differences of country-level numbers. They close. For the Sato–Vartia index this becomes an if-and-only-if, which is Abe and Rao’s Proposition 2 below; here it is only the reason to expect trouble.

There is a second way to see the failure, and it is the one I want to carry into the rest of the chapter.

5.3The loop view

Put every bilateral log index into one vector. With four countries there are six pairs, so t lives in R6, one coordinate per edge of the complete graph on four vertices (Figure 5.2). Orient each edge from its earlier letter to its later one.

Not every vector in R6 comes from price levels. The ones that do are the gradients: given log levels x=(xA,xB,xC,xD), the index on edge jk is xk−xj. Write that as Dx, where D is the 6×4 matrix with −1 and +1 in the row of each edge. Three things follow, and they are the Chapter 2 material in the smallest case where it matters.

  • The gradients form a subspace of dimension 3, not 4. Adding one constant to every x changes nothing; only ratios of price levels are observable. That is the familiar fact that no one can measure the world’s price level, only how far apart countries are.

  • The orthogonal complement has dimension 6−3=3. It is the loop space: vectors that circulate around closed routes, with as much arriving at each country as leaving it. The gap of a vector t around a loop is its inner product with that loop’s vector, so t closes around every loop exactly when it is orthogonal to the whole loop space, that is, when it is a gradient. In general a complete graph on C countries has (C2)−(C−1)=(C−1)(C−2)/2 independent loops. For the eight countries of the experiment below that is 21.

  • Four triangles do not give four independent loops. The triangle gaps satisfy gABC−gABD+gACD−gBCD=0, because every edge appears in two of the four triangles with opposite orientations and cancels. With the numbers above, −0.100−(−0.046)+(−0.049)−(−0.103)=0 to the printed precision. Three loops suffice, and any three of the four triangles will do.

Every vector t then splits in exactly one way into a gradient and a loop part, t=Dx+r with r⊥ every gradient. The gradient part is the part of the bilateral comparisons that price levels can explain. The loop part r is what they cannot. Circularity holds exactly when r=0. Everything that follows is about how to choose x.

5.4GEKS: project onto the gradients

Gini, Eltető and Köves, and Szulc proposed an answer that official statistics adopted: replace the index from j to k by the geometric mean, over all countries l, of the two-step route through l, GEKSjk=(∏l=1CFjlFlk)1/C,Fjj=1.GEKSjk=(∏l=1CFjlFlk)1/C,Fjj=1. Figure 5.3 unrolls the formula for A to D in the example. The route through A is just the direct index, and so is the route through D, so the direct index enters twice. The routes through B and C give 1.309 and 1.305. The geometric mean of 1.371,1.371,1.309,1.305 is 1.339.

Why this formula? Because it is the least-squares fit of a set of price levels to the Fisher indexes, and a few lines show it. Choose log levels x to minimize the sum of squared misfits over the edges, ∑j<k(tjk−(xk−xj))2. Set the derivative with respect to xk to zero. Because t is antisymmetric, the edges touching k contribute ∑ltlk=∑l(xk−xl)=Cxk−∑lxl. The levels are only determined up to a constant, so normalize ∑lxl=0, and then xk=1C∑ltlk. The log price level of country k is the average of the log Fisher indexes into k, taken from every country. The fitted index from j to k is xk−xj=1C∑l(tlk−tlj), and because tlj=−tjl this is 1C∑l(tjl+tlk), the logarithm of the GEKS formula. On the complete graph, then, GEKS is the least-squares gradient fit. The script that made the example checks it on the data above: the largest difference between the GEKS parities and a direct projection, computed with a pseudo-inverse of D, is 1.1×10−16.

Because GEKS is a difference of levels it is transitive by construction. Table 5.3 shows the Fisher indexes next to the parities they are replaced by; GEKS moves each one by up to about five percent.

The picture

The smallest loop is a triangle, and its edge space is three-dimensional, which means it can be drawn. Chapter 2 draws this picture for a triangle of roads; here it is drawn afresh for three countries. Take the edges in the order AB, BC, CA, so that a vector is (tAB,tBC,tCA). A gradient has the form (xB−xA, xC−xB, xA−xC), whose coordinates sum to zero. So the gradients fill the plane tAB+tBC+tCA=0, and the direction perpendicular to it is the diagonal (1,1,1): that is the loop direction, and the distance of a point from the plane, measured along the diagonal, is its loop gap divided by 3.

Figure 5.4 puts in the three Fisher indexes of triangle ABC, t=(−0.096, 0.291, −0.295). The point lies off the plane by |gABC|/3≈0.058. Its foot on the plane is t−g3(1,1,1), which is the GEKS parity of a three-country comparison: each of the three edges is moved by the same amount, −g/3. For the three countries alone the GEKS index from A to B is therefore 0.908×e−g/3=0.939. With the fourth country in the data the parity is 0.942. Adding D moved the A-to-B comparison, because GEKS levels are averages over every country present. That is a feature of any least-squares fit on a complete graph, and a nuisance in practice: a published parity between two countries changes when a third joins.

GEKS is the orthogonal projection of the bilateral indexes onto the plane of gradients, and the short vertical segment is the residual that it discards. Hill’s proposal is another way to make the comparison transitive. Chain the bilateral indexes along a spanning tree of the countries and use no other edge. A tree has no loops, so every vector on it is a gradient, exactly as in Chapter 8, where tables on a tree always glue; the price is that all the other comparisons, and their disagreement with the chain, are thrown away unexamined. GEKS uses every edge and discards only the loop part. Both make circularity hold by deleting the evidence that it failed.

5.5What the papers say

We read the papers in this section at very different depths, and say so each time.

What the paper says: Gini; Eltető and Köves; Szulc, the GEKS method

We read a summary of the method and did not read the three originals. What the summary says is the formula above: transitive multilateral indexes built from non-transitive bilateral ones by geometric averaging over all intermediate countries. The least-squares reading of the formula is not something we took from those sources; the project’s experiment, described below, confirmed it numerically, and the three lines above derive it.

What the paper says: Statistics Netherlands discussion paper (2017), “Transitivizing elementary price indices for internet data using the cycle method”

We read the summary section and Section 2 only. The paper draws a “price index digraph” and says that around a cycle the product of indexes should be one; its “cycle method” generalizes GEKS. It treats failure of transitivity as a defect to remove. In the passages we read, it does not interpret the size of the failure.

What the paper says: Hill (1999), Review of Economics and Statistics 81(1)

We read a summary. Hill chains bilateral comparisons along the spanning tree that minimizes the Paasche–Laspeyres spread. Later work by Hajargasht, Hill, Rao and Shankar, again as summarized, calls the pure tree too extreme and chains along shortest paths instead. Nothing we read says how much the choice of tree matters for any particular data set.

What the paper says: Abe and Rao (2019), “Multilateral Sato–Vartia Index for International Comparisons of Prices and Real Expenditures”

This one we read in full except the proofs in the appendix. The paper explains why the Sato–Vartia index, exact for common CES preferences, had not been used for multilateral comparison: it is not transitive. It proves that it is transitive if and only if its weights do not depend on the pair compared (Proposition 2), proposes three ways to choose pair-invariant weights close to the observed ones, and applies them to the 2011 International Comparison Program data. It also notes that GEKS gives the transitive index closest to a given binary index in the least-squares sense. Their first distance measure, the root-mean-square log distance between each of their four transitive Sato–Vartia indexes and the binary one, is 0.046 to 0.048 for 47 countries and 139 basic headings. The paper treats intransitivity as something to repair and uses the distance to rank repairs; in the sections we read it does not ask what the distance says about the countries.

What the paper says: Redding and Weinstein (2020), “Measuring Aggregate Price Indices with Taste Shocks”

We read the main text in full as extracted prose, with garbled equations and no online appendix. The paper works over time, not across countries, and a text search of it found nothing on transitivity or circularity. For this chapter one number matters. They report that the standard deviation of the difference between the Sato–Vartia and Fisher indexes is only 0.06 percentage points a year. That is a comparison of two indexes along one time series, not of loop consistency across places, and Section 5.6 returns to why the two can differ. Chapter 6 takes up the rest of the paper.

What the paper says: “The Empirical Welfare Content of International Price and Income Comparisons,” arXiv:2504.16654

We read the summary only. It argues that GEKS parities are transitive but need not be consistent with a cost-of-living interpretation, finds that 3 to 5 percent of country pairs fall outside non-homothetic bounds, and proposes taste-corrected values. We cannot say more about its method.

What the paper says: Malaney and Weinstein, and Nguyen’s response, arXiv:2112.03460

Summary only. Malaney and Weinstein recast index numbers as parallel transport for a connection, a gauge-theory picture. Nguyen’s response argues that the main corollary is a tautology and that a conjecture is false as stated. I mention it because this chapter is also a geometric restatement of index numbers, and the standard that the criticism sets is the right one for it: the picture earns its place only by predicting or computing something the plain formulas do not.

5.6What we found

Research cycle 2 asked whether the size of the circularity failure carries information about differences in tastes across countries. The answer belongs to Chapter 6. What belongs here is what the experiment (exp2_price_index_circularity.py) showed about the indexes themselves.

Design. Eight countries, twenty goods, CES consumers with elasticity σ=4, log prices with standard deviation 0.3, taste shifters with a common component of standard deviation 0.5 plus a country-specific component of standard deviation τ. Two hundred economies for each of five values of τ. The complete graph on eight countries has 28 edges and 21 independent loops. For each index the script computes the share of squared variation that does not glue, that is ∥r∥2/∥t∥2 with r the loop part of the index vector; the table averages it, and the root-mean-square size of r, over the 200 economies. Because it is a ratio of squared lengths, a share of 0.109 means that the loop part is about a third of the length of the vector (0.109≈0.33).

The Sato–Vartia column is the subject of Chapter 6 and serves here as a control: it is exactly transitive when tastes are common and breaks only as tastes diverge.

Three results. First, GEKS is the least-squares gradient fit. In every one of the 200 economies at each of the five values of τ, the GEKS parities and the projection computed with a pseudo-inverse agreed to at most 3.3×10−16, which is rounding. This confirms the derivation of the previous section on a case larger than the example. Second, with identical tastes in every country, 3.5 percent of the Törnqvist index’s squared variation and 10.9 percent of the Fisher index’s does not glue. Nothing about tastes is going on in those economies. The failure comes from the formulas: neither index is exact for these CES preferences. Third, the failure grows with taste dispersion for all three indexes, but the Fisher residual starts high and rises slowly, from 10.9 to 18.2 percent as τ goes from 0 to 0.8.

The second result is the one to carry away. Official parities are built from Fisher indexes, and a loop residual in a set of Fisher indexes therefore cannot be read as evidence of taste differences: in this design most of it is formula error. Whatever the loop part of a Fisher index measures, it does not cleanly measure anything about preferences.

A mistake and its correction. After first reading Abe and Rao we wrote in the notebook that all their transitive variants correlate above 0.999, which suggested that the circularity residual is small in practice and that matched the small residuals of our simulation. A second reading, this time of their Table 2, showed that the note had the wrong object. The correlations are among six transitive indexes, four Sato–Vartia repairs and two GEKS, and repairs of nearly the same comparisons naturally agree. What bears on the residual is the distance between a transitive index and the binary one. GEKS applied to the Sato–Vartia index is the closest transitive index, so its distance would be the residual itself; Abe and Rao report the distance only for their four Sato–Vartia repairs, about 0.047 in logs, and that bounds the residual from above. Our simulation’s average Sato–Vartia residual at its highest taste dispersion was 0.036. So the correlations cannot show that the residual in real ICP data is small, and the distances that bound it are of the same size as the simulation’s at its highest dispersion. A real data set also has everything the simulation left out: basic headings much coarser than goods, different goods in different countries, measurement error. We would not draw a conclusion from the comparison beyond that.

Redding and Weinstein’s 0.06 points a year needs the same care. In the simulation the Fisher index fails circularity far more than the Sato–Vartia index, while over time the two track each other closely. Those are different questions, how far two indexes differ along one route and whether each closes around loops, and both answers can be true at once.

Limits of the design. Every country in the simulation buys the same twenty goods; real countries do not, and Chapter 7 is about what happens on the overlap. No sampling error enters prices or shares, so nothing here says what it takes to detect a loop gap in data. And the whole construction rests on CES with a single elasticity, which is why only the Sato–Vartia column has a clean interpretation. What the loop view contributes is a way of seeing the repair that official statistics has used for decades. It does not contribute a new result, and I do not want to suggest otherwise.
