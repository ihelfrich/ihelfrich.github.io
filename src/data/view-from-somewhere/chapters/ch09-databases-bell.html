Chapter 9

Databases and Bell’s theorem

Web edition. Figures 9.2 and 9.3 are live: you can load other tables and move the point in the slice.

Here is an invented database, small enough to hold in your head. A research institute keeps three tables. Works-in says which office each person sits in, Hosts says which project each office hosts, and Staffs says which project each person is on.

Works-in Hosts Staffs
Person Office Office Project Person Project
Ann North North p Ann q
Bo South South q Bo p

Compare the tables one pair at a time. Works-in and Staffs name the same two people. Works-in and Hosts name the same two offices. Hosts and Staffs name the same two projects. No pair contradicts another about who or what exists. And yet no single table of (person, office, project) triples can be the source of all three. Ann sits in the North; the North hosts p; but Staffs puts Ann on q. Pairwise the tables agree, and as a whole they cannot be one table.

You have seen this object before. Replace each table by the question “do the two columns match, row for row?” Works-in matches people to offices one to one, Hosts matches offices to projects, and Staffs matches people to projects with the pairing crossed. The three disagreement rates are (a,b,d)=(0,0,1), and d≤a+b fails, which is the first face of the tetrahedron in Chapter 8. This chapter is about the paper that showed why a database administrator and a quantum physicist keep meeting this object, why the matching literature meets it too (our reading, not the paper’s), and what each of them has learned about it. By the end you should be able to say, for any collection of tables, whether the question “is there one table behind these?” can fail at all, and if it can, which of three increasingly severe ways it fails in.

9.1Consistent in pairs, impossible as a whole

Fix some vocabulary from databases. An attribute is a column name: Person, Office, Project. A table on a set of attributes is a set of rows, each row a value for every attribute in the set. The set of attribute sets a database uses is its schema; the tables themselves, one per set, are an instance. Project a table onto some of its columns by deleting the others and removing duplicate rows. Two tables are consistent if they give the same table when both are projected onto the attributes they share. In Abramsky’s words (Section 2.3) an instance whose tables are consistent in every pair is a compatible family.

The question we care about is the one Abramsky calls the gluing condition: is there one table on all the attributes whose projection onto each set in the schema is the table we were given? Such a table is a universal relation. Consistency in pairs is necessary, since projections of one big table onto overlapping sets must agree on the overlap. It is not sufficient, and the staffing database is the smallest case we could find.

The join is the machine that tests it. Join Works-in and Hosts: keep every (person, office, project) triple whose first two entries are a Works-in row and whose last two are a Hosts row. Here that gives two triples, (Ann, North, p) and (Bo, South, q). Project them onto (person, project) and you get (Ann, p) and (Bo, q), which is not Staffs. Abramsky’s Proposition 2.3 says that is the whole test: a universal relation exists if and only if the join of all the tables projects back onto each of them, and then the join is the largest universal relation. A join that does not project back is the sign that none exists, and ours loses Staffs entirely.

Two more things are worth knowing about universal relations. They need not be unique when they exist: two tables that share one column are glued by many different relations, which in Chapter 8 was the size of the set of joints that fit two surveys. And deciding whether one exists is hard. The database name for the question is join consistency, and Abramsky reports, on the strength of Honeyman, Ladner and Yannakakis (1980), that it is NP-complete. We have not read that paper. We will come back to what the hardness does and does not mean in the last section of ideas.

9.2A dictionary

Abramsky’s observation is that the same object has been studied under other names, and the dictionary below, whose first two columns are his table in Section 7 with probability tables added to the fifth row, lets the reader read a result in one literature as a result in another. The third column is ours.

Relational databases Measurement scenarios Surveys (our addition)
attribute measurement a question
set of attributes of a table compatible set of measurements, a context the questions one survey asks
database schema measurement cover the plan of which surveys overlap
tuple local section: a joint outcome one respondent’s answers to one survey
relation, or probability table Boolean or probabilistic distribution on joint outcomes a table a survey reports
universal relation global section, a hidden-variable model one population answering every question
acyclicity Vorob’ev condition no loop of overlapping surveys

The word that does the work is cover. Think of a set of attributes as a patch, a region of the whole set of questions that can be looked at together, and the schema as a collection of patches that between them cover everything. A table on a patch is what you can see through it. Projection is restriction: look through a smaller patch inside the big one and you see less. Doing it in two steps, first to a middle patch and then to a smaller one, gives what doing it in one step gives; that is all “presheaf” means here. For probability tables the restriction is marginalization, and Abramsky says so in Section 3, where he replaces sets of tuples by functions from tuples into a semiring. We will use that freedom in the last section.

A sheaf is a presheaf with a further promise. Given views on patches that agree wherever patches overlap, there is a view of the union that restricts to each of them, and exactly one. Tables break both halves. Uniqueness fails in general, as in matching, where the shared column does not determine the others. Existence fails in the staffing database. Abramsky puts it tersely: the relational presheaf is not a sheaf. The word compatible is the hypothesis of the promise, agreement on overlaps. The word global section is its conclusion. In physics the same agreement on overlaps is the no-signaling condition: whether Alice measures her setting together with one of Bob’s or with the other, her own outcomes must look the same. The paper notes that every table from quantum mechanics satisfies it.

It is worth saying why anyone would want a physicist’s name for this. A table in a measurement scenario is a distribution over the joint outcomes of measurements that can be made on the same run. Measurements that cannot be made together never share a table. A hidden-variable theory that is local and realistic says that every measurement has a definite outcome on each run, whether or not it is made, so there is a distribution over all of them at once, and each table is its marginal. That distribution is a global section. A survey respondent has the same structure: each respondent has an answer to every question, but one survey asks only some of them, so the tables are marginals of one population if and only if a global section exists.

9.3Trees glue and loops need not

Look at the shape of the cover, as in Chapter 8, where two surveys were a tree and three were a loop.

The precise statement has two halves, and the notes record where each one comes from. The first is Vorob’ev’s (1962): there is a combinatorial condition on a schema under which every compatible family of probability tables has a global section, and no weaker condition will do. Abramsky passes on, as a personal communication from Rui Soares Barbosa, that the condition is equivalent to acyclicity in the database sense, and we take it on that authority, since the equivalence is not proved in the paper. The second half is the converse: Atserias and Kolaitis (2024) show that on a cyclic schema there are always pairwise-consistent tables that do not glue, and that acyclic covers glue exactly when the values the tables take admit solutions to every balanced transportation problem. Booleans, non-negative integers and non-negative reals qualify. So for tables of rows, tables of counts and tables of probabilities alike, a loop in the cover is exactly the condition for trouble to be possible.

We have not read Vorob’ev, and we have only the introduction of Atserias and Kolaitis. Here is the argument we would give for why acyclic covers glue, which is the matching construction of Chapter 8 done repeatedly. We state acyclicity the way database texts do, from memory and not from a source in this project (Abramsky cites Beeri, Fagin, Maier and Yannakakis (1983) for it): a cover is acyclic if you can keep removing a set S whose overlap with all the other sets together lies inside a single other set T, until nothing is left. The chain in Figure 9.1 comes apart this way: remove {A,B}, whose overlap with the rest is {B}, inside {B,C,D}. Now suppose the other sets have been glued into one joint q. It restricts to the given table on T. The table on S agrees with the table on T over S∩T, since the family is compatible, and so it agrees with q over S’s overlap with the rest, which sits inside S∩T. Two tables agreeing on what they share can always be glued, by pairing their private columns at random given the shared ones; this is the construction of the first section of Chapter 8. So S can be put back, and induction does the rest. A loop is exactly what stops the process: no set can be removed first, because each one’s overlap is split between two neighbors, and nothing forces those two neighbors to agree with each other through the far side of the loop.

One consequence deserves to be stated plainly, because it concerns the design of a study and not its data. Acyclicity is a property of the cover. If the surveys form a tree, no compatible set of reports can ever show that the population is impossible; there is nothing to test. Whatever coherence test you want must come from the loops. That is what “every test of coherence lives on the cycles” meant in the reading notes, and it explains why Chapter 8’s tree of two surveys had a band and no test. It is also a fact about the cover and not about the data, and adding a table can change it: put a fourth table on all three attributes of the triangle and the cover is acyclic again. That table is itself a universal relation, and the three pairwise tables are then its marginals if they are consistent with it; the loop has been resolved by observing the whole.

9.4Bell’s four-cycle

Now the case that gives the paper its title. Alice chooses between two measurements, a and a′, and Bob between b and b′. Each measurement has outcomes 0 and 1. Alice and Bob each choose one, so the contexts are the four pairs (a,b), (a′,b), (a,b′) and (a′,b′), and the cover is the four-cycle of Figure 9.1: a and a′ never share a table, and neither do b and b′. Abramsky’s Section 5.1 takes the empirical model

(0,0) (1,0) (0,1) (1,1)
(a,b) 1/2 0 0 1/2
(a,b′) 3/8 1/8 1/8 3/8
(a′,b) 3/8 1/8 1/8 3/8
(a′,b′) 1/8 3/8 3/8 1/8

where an entry (x,y) means Alice’s outcome is x and Bob’s is y. The paper says it can be realized with a Bell state and measurements in the XY-plane at a relative angle of π/3; we take that on the paper’s word, since quantum mechanics is not what we are studying. Figure 9.2 draws the four tables on the four-cycle, the positive entries shaded.

Every pair of tables that share an attribute agree on it; every margin is 12. The family is compatible. Abramsky’s Proposition 5.1 says there is no global section: no distribution on the 16 assignments of 0 or 1 to (a,a′,b,b′) restricts to all four tables. His proof writes the 16 weights as X1,…,X16 and uses four of the 16 equations. The first says the weights of the assignments with a=b=0 add to 12. Three others, added, say that a larger collection of weights, which contains those four and counts one of them twice, adds to 38. Weights are non-negative, and a part cannot exceed the whole: 38≥12.

We find the same obstruction easier to see in the language of Chapter 8, and this rewriting is ours. Look at disagreements. If a′≠b′ in some run, then walking round the other way, from a′ to b to a to b′, two neighbors must differ somewhere, since the two ends differ. So P(a′≠b′)≤P(a′≠b)+P(b≠a)+P(a≠b′): the triangle inequality d≤a+b with one more edge in the sum. The tables give P(a′≠b′)=34≰14+0+14=12. The four-cycle has four of these loop inequalities, one for each edge, and Abramsky’s table breaks one of them. Physicists write the same thing with correlations E=P(agree)−P(differ). Here Eab=1, Eab′=Ea′b=12 and Ea′b′=−12, and any global section forces |Eab+Eab′+Ea′b−Ea′b′|≤2, the CHSH form, because each of the 16 assignments gives this combination the value +2 or −2 and a mixture cannot leave [−2,2]. Our table gives 1+12+12+12=2.5. Abramsky’s paper does not write the CHSH form. Chapter 8 wrote the three-cycle version of it, and the notes record that Kujala, Dzhafarov and Larsson prove the version for every cycle, which is the subject of Chapter 11.

Two more numbers say how far from gluing this table is, both from a linear program of ours (figures/ch09_bell.py). The smallest total absolute change in the 16 table entries that makes the four tables glue, keeping each a probability table, is 12. And if you allow negative weights, a signed global section exists, as Abramsky and Brandenburger prove for every compatible family; the smallest total negative weight it needs is 18. That is the obstruction in a different currency: what fails is not that the linear equations have no solution but that their solutions are not probabilities. We come back to this in Section 9.6.

The simplest local polytope, in three dimensions

Chapter 8 had a tetrahedron inside a cube. The four-cycle has the same structure one dimension up, and we can show a slice of it honestly. Restrict to tables with uniform margins, so that each table is fixed by its correlation: the cube [−1,1]4 of all compatible families of that kind. The ones that glue form a polytope inside it, cut out by the four CHSH forms. Our check on 3,000 random points of the cube agrees with a direct linear program on every one of them, and about two thirds (0.677) of the cube glues. Now take the three-dimensional slice where Eab′=Ea′b=m, so the coordinates are (Eab,m,Ea′b′), which contains Abramsky’s point (1,12,−12).

The slice is the cube with four corners sliced off, each by a plane through two of the corner’s neighbors and the midpoint of the edge to the third. It has six vertices and eight faces, and its volume is 16/3 against 8 for the cube. Both the Bell point and the corner (1,1,−1) are outside it. That corner is the table in which (a,b), (a,b′) and (a′,b) agree perfectly and (a′,b′) disagrees perfectly; Chapter 8’s triangle has the same kind of corner, (1,1,1) in disagreements, which is not in the tetrahedron either. The cube is the same thing in both chapters, the compatible families with uniform margins. Physicists call the corner here a Popescu–Rohrlich box, a name we know from outside the sources of this project.

9.5Three strengths of failure

Bell’s table fails to glue, but it fails mildly. Look at its supports. The table (a,b) has support {(0,0),(1,1)} and the other three have full support. A relation on four attributes whose projections are these supports is easy to find: take every assignment with a=b. So the support of Bell’s table glues even though the table does not. Abramsky points this out after Proposition 5.3. It is one of the three strengths.

Hardy. His Section 5.2 gives a table whose zeros alone refute every global section. Here are its supports, shown in Figure 9.4. In (a,b) every outcome is possible. In (a′,b) and in (a,b′) the outcome (0,0) is not. In (a′,b′) the outcome (1,1) is not. Suppose a universal relation R existed. Since (a,b)=(0,0) is possible, some tuple of R has a=0 and b=0. Then a′=0 is impossible, since (a′,b)=(0,0) is forbidden, so a′=1; and b′=0 is impossible, since (a,b′)=(0,0) is forbidden, so b′=1; and (a′,b′)=(1,1) is forbidden. No such tuple exists, so R does not. Abramsky writes the same argument as a satisfiability problem over the Booleans. Our check by enumeration agrees: of the 16 assignments, five survive all four supports, and none has a=b=0.

This is stronger than Bell. Hardy’s argument uses only which outcomes are possible and not how probable, so it refutes a probabilistic hidden variable model too (Proposition 5.3); Bell’s does not go the other way. The table in the paper is a support table. We wanted to see that a probability table with exactly Hardy’s support and consistent margins exists, and found one by linear programming, in sevenths; it is ours and not the paper’s. Its rows are (17,17,17,47) for (a,b), (0,27,47,17) for (a′,b), (0,47,27,17) for (a,b′) and (17,37,37,0) for (a′,b′), in the column order above. It does not glue, by the argument just given, and Figure 9.2 confirms it once that table is loaded.

GHZ. Hardy’s support still has one global tuple that fits in every table, for instance a=1, a′=0, b=1, b′=0; the paper notes this. Call a model strongly contextual if even that fails: not a single global assignment projects into every table. Abramsky’s Proposition 6.1 shows that the GHZ models, with n≥3 parties and two measurements each, are strongly contextual, and the argument for n=3 is simple enough to do here. Each party has X and Y. The rule for a context is that the outcomes have an even number of 1s if it has no Y⁠s, and an odd number if it has two. Write the outcomes as bits x1,x2,x3 and y1,y2,y3 and add mod 2 the three constraints for the contexts XYY, YXY and YYX (each demands an odd sum): (x1+y2+y3)+(y1+x2+y3)+(y1+y2+x3)≡1+1+1. Each yi appears twice and cancels, leaving x1+x2+x3≡1, odd. But the context XXX demands even. So no global tuple exists. The derivation is ours; the paper adapts Mermin’s case analysis and says that all eight assignments satisfying the three constraints violate the fourth, which our enumeration confirms.

Kochen–Specker. The same idea at scale. Suppose each attribute takes values 0 or 1 and every table’s support is the set of tuples with exactly one 1. A global tuple is then a choice of attributes to set to 1 so that each set in the cover contains exactly one of them. Abramsky’s Proposition 6.2 notes that if a global tuple exists, then every common divisor of the numbers of sets that contain each attribute divides the number of sets, since the sets split into the groups of those containing each chosen attribute. He takes the 18-vector schema from the Kochen–Specker literature: nine sets of four attributes, eighteen attributes in all, each in exactly two sets. Then 2 divides every count but not 9, and there is no global tuple. We reconstructed the schema from the paper’s printed table and checked that every attribute indeed lies in two sets; Figure 9.5 draws it. Draw each set as a vertex and each attribute, being in two sets, as an edge between them. A global tuple is then a set of edges that touches every vertex exactly once, a perfect matching, and no graph on an odd number of vertices has one. That reading of the proof as a matching problem is ours. A brute-force search over all 218 choices confirms it.

9.6The semiring as a dial

So far we have used two kinds of table without comment: tables of rows, and tables of probabilities. Abramsky’s Section 3 shows that they are one construction run in two different arithmetics. A table is a function from tuples to some commutative semiring S, with finitely many non-zero values. Projection adds weights over the tuples that agree on the retained columns. In the Booleans, with OR for addition and AND for multiplication, a table is a set of rows, and projection is the relational projection from the start of the chapter. In the non-negative reals, a table whose weights sum to 1 is a probability distribution and projection is marginalization. The paper lists the min-plus semiring too, and notes that provenance semirings are a database topic; we use only the first two.

The map that sends each positive probability to 1 and keeps zero as zero is the unique semiring homomorphism from the non-negative reals to the Booleans. It is the support. Because it respects addition (a sum of non-negative numbers is positive exactly when some term is), it commutes with projection, so the support of a marginal is the projection of the support. It follows at once that a probabilistic global section has a Boolean one as its support. The contrapositive is how Hardy’s table is refuted: show that the support does not glue, and no choice of the probabilities can. The converse is false; Bell’s table is the counterexample, since its support glues and it does not. The three rows of the table above are three places in this implication where the dial can be stuck.

There is a third setting of the dial, and it is the one that explains the rest. Over all the real numbers, with negative weights allowed, the linear system that asks for a global section always has a solution for a compatible family. That is Abramsky and Brandenburger’s Theorems 5.5 and 5.9, as our notes have them from the dissection of the 2011 paper, and our Bell calculation above is an instance: it needs total negative weight 18 and no more. So in probability tables everything glues linearly. The obstruction is the sign constraint, an inequality and not an equation. This is the precise sense in which the present problem differs from the least-squares chapters of Parts I and II, where the obstruction was a failed linear equation, and why the test of Chapter 10 projects onto a cone and not a subspace. The authors cite Feynman for treating a negative probability as a calculational device that signals the conditions cannot all be realized together.

The cost of the dial is its price in computation. A table cover on n binary attributes has 2n possible global assignments, so the linear program has 2n unknowns, which is a remark of ours and not the paper’s. The paper’s own statement is that deciding the Boolean question, the join consistency property, is NP-complete. For the probability case Vantaggi’s coherence checks are also NP-complete in general, in the notes’ account of her paper. Neither fact is a barrier for a loop. For a cycle of two-valued properties the question has a closed form in Kujala, Dzhafarov and Larsson (as the notes record it), and for the triangle it was solved in Chapter 8 with four inequalities. The hardness is for arbitrary covers. Which covers arise in survey and administrative data is an empirical matter and not one this project has settled.

9.7What the papers say

What the paper says: Abramsky (2013), “Relational databases and Bell’s theorem”

Read in full from the stored arXiv text, nineteen pages. It sets out the dictionary of Section 2 and the table of Section 7, proves that a universal relation is a solution of the gluing condition (Proposition 2.2) and equivalent to a lossless join (2.3), generalizes tables to any semiring, and then re-proves Bell’s, Hardy’s, the GHZ and the Kochen–Specker results as failures of global sections of increasing strength. It states Vorob’ev’s condition as a link to acyclicity and credits the equivalence to Barbosa. What it does not do is considerable. It is an introductory paper presenting known results as a correspondence; by its own account, sheaf cohomology for databases, logical Bell inequalities in the database setting and categories of instances are left as suggestions. It has nothing about estimation, noise or sample sizes, so that where we asked for a test of the loop, the paper gives only the existence question. It does not mention the CHSH inequality, statistical matching, or economic data.

What the paper says: Abramsky and Brandenburger (2011), “The sheaf-theoretic structure of non-locality and contextuality”

The first paper of the pair, and the one Abramsky (2013) builds on. Read on November 11, 2026: introduction and postlude in full, Sections 2 and 3 selectively, the statement of every main theorem and proposition in Sections 3 to 9; the proofs, the section on Kochen–Specker graphs and most of the section on quantum representations were not read. The results cited here, the real-valued solvability, the extension of hidden variables from deterministic to random (Theorem 8.1) and the strict hierarchy of strengths (Proposition 6.1), are from the statements we read. The library table in the project says this paper was “not read” and “cited from memory”; that row predates the dissection and is out of date. Brandenburger is a game theorist, and the notes find that the setup maps onto choice data without change.

What the paper says: Vorob’ev (1962); Honeyman, Ladner and Yannakakis (1980); Beeri, Fagin, Maier and Yannakakis (1983)

Not read. Everything this chapter says about them is Abramsky’s report of them in Sections 2.3 and 7, which is a pointer and not evidence about what the original papers prove. The proof of acyclic gluing in this chapter is our own reconstruction, not Vorob’ev’s.

What the paper says: Atserias and Kolaitis (2024)

Read in part: the arXiv version’s introduction and the statement of its Theorem 2. Proofs not read. The statement of the transportation property and the converse for cyclic schemas are taken from there, as the notes record them. Gluing two tables along shared columns is a balanced transportation problem with no cost function; that observation, which connects gluing to optimal-transport coupling, is the notes’ reading of the paper and not a sentence we verified in it.

What the paper says: Vantaggi (2005); D’Orazio, Di Zio and Scanu (2006)

Vantaggi was read through her Theorem 4 (Sections 1 to 4); her worked example and comparison were not. She puts matching in the language of de Finetti coherence, and the notes record that with no logical constraint linking the two questions that never share a survey the assessment is always coherent. Her example of a constraint that breaks this is that under-18s hold no driver’s license: for residents of the first of her two municipalities, P(under 18)=722 in one file and P(license)=34 in the other, so the two disjoint events would have probability 722+34=4744>1, our arithmetic. D’Orazio and coauthors study the size of the set of joints in the matching problem; we read Sections 1 to 4, 5.2, 6 and the appendix, not their worked example. Section 5.2 has a structural zero that contradicts the Fréchet lower bound from the estimated margins, which is the empty set arising in practice. Neither paper cites Vorob’ev or the database literature in the passages read.

9.8What we found

This chapter sits on research cycle 4, in which the question was whether statistical matching, which combines surveys that share some variables, is the universal-relation problem of Abramsky (2013), and if so what the loop adds. The notebook’s answer, which the memo repeats, is a translation and not a result.

The translation, with the numbers that went with it. For binary X,Y,Z with uniform margins, a joint exists exactly when |a−b|≤d≤min(a+b,2−a−b). The closed form agreed with a linear program at all 9,261 grid points of (a,b,d). On the tree {XY,XZ} the distance from the reports to the set that glues was 0 for every a and b tried, which is the tree theorem of Section 9.3 observed on a grid. The share of third surveys that would be caught at a=b=0.05,…,0.5 is 0.90, 0.80, 0.60, 0.40, 0.20, 0.00, the table of Chapter 8: matching’s uncertainty and a test’s power are the same width of the same band. The notebook also records two things worth carrying: that uncertainty–power duality as a way to choose which third survey to field, and the cover with probability tables on two edges and a possibility table on the third, which the reading notes did not find in what was read of either literature. The last is Vantaggi’s under-18 example, with the disjointness of two events as the Boolean table on the closing edge.

Then the first attempt at a test, which the notebook is candid about. It used the L1 distance from the sample tables to the set that glues, with a critical value taken from one boundary point, d=0.4, of one family (a=b=0.2): 0.168 at n=500 per survey and 0.087 at n=2000. With 1,000 replications:

With weak links, a=b=0.4 and d=0.6 at n=2000, the test rejected in only 0.031 of replications: a survey from a quite different population passes. No simulated sample ever glued exactly, since sampling error alone breaks the one-way margins, so “do the tables glue?” cannot be asked of raw samples.

The red-team notes then listed what was wrong with this. The critical value was ad hoc: one boundary point of one family, not a least-favorable bound, so the size figures were illustrative only. The L1 distance mixes margin noise with loop violation. The last column of Chapter 8’s table (cycle 5, with the loop part of the statistic) rejects 0.400 at d=0.45 and n=500 where this table’s rejects 0.249, which fits that diagnosis without proving it: the two are different statistics with different critical values, and cycle 5’s full statistic, which also mixes the two parts, rejects only 0.145 there. Chapter 10 is about the loop part, the one that works. And binary variables with uniform margins are the easiest case. The notebook’s verdict: matching is the gluing problem on a tree; the statistics literature has studied the size of the solution set in depth without the sheaf or database vocabulary; nothing new is established.

One thing about the reading itself. The first entry on Abramsky in the reading notes was written from a summary and said, in two lines, that existence of a local hidden-variable model is existence of a universal relation. The entry that replaced it, from the full text, added what we have used in this chapter: the three levels, the support homomorphism and its false converse, the NP-completeness pointer, and the fact that the equivalence of Vorob’ev’s condition and acyclicity is a personal communication and not a proved theorem. The difference did not change the translation, and it did change what the memo may say about acyclicity.

Nothing here is a test on data. The question the research inherits from this chapter is the one Abramsky leaves untouched: what can be concluded from tables that are only estimated, and where the answer depends on how near the truth is to a face of one of these polytopes. Chapter 10 takes up the first half of that and Part IV the second.
