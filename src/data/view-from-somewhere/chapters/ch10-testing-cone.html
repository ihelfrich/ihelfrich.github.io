Chapter 10

Testing a cone

Web edition. Figure 10.2 is live: you can move the truth, change the wedge and the sample size, and draw again.

Chapter 8 ended with a rule that accused an honest city. Take a=b=0.2 and a third survey whose true disagreement rate is d=0.38, just inside the face of the tetrahedron, with 500 people per survey. The rule said that a report outside the tetrahedron means no population exists, and it fired on 79 of 300 simulated sets of surveys. What we need is a critical value: a statement of how far outside the tetrahedron a report must land before noise stops being a good explanation. Econometrics already has one, built for a neighboring problem. Its central move is odd enough to carry this chapter. To test whether a point lies in a set, it deliberately shrinks the set.

By the end you will have seen the set as a cone, the statistic as a distance to the cone, the reason why a bootstrap that simply resamples the data cannot give the critical value, the repair that Kitamura and Stoye call a double recentering, and an exact split of the statistic into two parts that turned out to matter for power. You will also see how the first run of the experiment went wrong, because the way it went wrong is instructive.

10.1The cone behind the tetrahedron

Chapter 8 described a population by four shares. The testing literature writes the same thing in a form that scales to many questions, and we need that form. Stack the twelve cell frequencies of the three two-by-two tables in one vector π∈R12: survey 1’s four cells, then survey 2’s, then survey 3’s. A population is a distribution ν over the eight answer strings (x,y,z)∈{0,1}3. A person who gives the string (1,0,1) lands in one cell of each table: the cell (X,Y)=(1,0) of table 1, the cell (X,Z)=(1,1) of table 2, the cell (Y,Z)=(0,1) of table 3. So each string has a footprint in R12, a vector with three ones. Put the eight footprints in the columns of a 12×8 matrix A. A population with weights ν≥0 produces the tables Aν, and the question of Chapter 8 becomes the question of whether π∈C:={Aν: ν≥0}, the set of all non-negative combinations of the eight footprints. A set of that form is a finitely generated cone. The tetrahedron of Chapter 8 is the slice of C on which all three margins equal one half.

Two facts about A matter later. First, its rank is 7 and not 8. The vector k that gives each string the sign (−1)x+y+z satisfies Ak=0: a pair table adds over the third answer, and the alternating signs cancel. That is the three-way interaction, the one thing no pair of questions can see, and it is the reason different populations can produce the same three tables. (This is the non-uniqueness the matching literature calls uncertainty.) Second, the cone lies in the seven-dimensional column space L of A, and five linear equations cut L out of R12. Two say that the three tables carry the same total mass, which sample frequencies satisfy automatically. Three say that the shared margins agree: X the same in tables 1 and 2, Y the same in tables 1 and 3, Z the same in tables 2 and 3. Sample margins from different samples almost never agree exactly, so a sample π^ almost always sits off L. Keep that in mind; Section 10.5 comes back to it.

The statistic is a squared distance. With π^ the twelve sample frequencies and N the number of people per survey (written n in the tables below, as in Chapter 8), J=Nminν≥0∥π^−Aν∥2, which is N times the squared distance from π^ to C. Kitamura and Stoye allow a positive diagonal weighting inside the norm. We use the identity, as the experiments in this chapter do. J=0 exactly when π^ itself lies in the cone.

10.2Distance to a cone

Figure 10.1 is a cone in three dimensions, invented for the purpose and drawn from computed coordinates. It has three generators, so it is a pyramid-shaped corner that goes on forever; the drawing cuts it off. The point π^ sits outside, in front of the face spanned by two of the generators. Its nearest point in the cone is η^, the foot of the perpendicular dropped on that face.

A point can have its nearest neighbor in four kinds of place. If π^ is inside, the nearest point is itself and the distance is zero. If it is outside and in front of a face, the nearest point is the foot of the perpendicular on that face, as in the figure. If it is out beyond an edge, the nearest point is on the edge, and if it is behind the apex, the nearest point is the apex. In the language of ν, which is how the computer sees it, the faces are where some weights are zero. In the survey problem, where several populations give the same tables, a face is a set of tables that no population can produce without leaving some answer strings empty, and a face of the tetrahedron is a kind of person running out, as in Chapter 8.

Now ask what J looks like when the truth is at one of these places. Write Z for noise of standard size, scaled so that π^=π0+Z/N. Everything below is about the limit.

  • If π0 is strictly inside C, then π^ is inside with probability tending to one and J=0.

  • If π0 is on one face and far from all the others, only the direction perpendicular to the face matters. J is then zero when the noise points inward, half the time, and a squared standard normal when it points outward: half a point mass at zero and half a χ12.

  • If π0 is at a place where two or more faces meet, J is a mixture of chi-squares of different degrees of freedom, and the weights depend on the angles between the faces. In the plane, take a wedge of opening α and put the truth at its apex. The noise lands inside the wedge with probability α/2π, giving J=0. It lands in the opposite region, the polar cone, with probability (π−α)/2π, giving a squared two-dimensional length, a χ22. Otherwise it lands in front of one of the two faces, with total probability one half, giving a χ12. For α=60∘ the weights on J=0, χ12 and χ22 are 1/6, 1/2 and 1/3. This is a calculation of ours, and Try it .1 asks you to repeat it.

So the null distribution of J is a mixture of chi-squares whose weights are set by how many faces the truth touches and at what angles. That alone would be an inconvenience. The trouble is that the mixture does not change smoothly as the truth moves. A face at distance c/N from the truth matters for every fixed c, and its influence fades only as c grows; no face at all, and the face itself, are different limits. Kitamura and Stoye say that the limit law depends discontinuously on where the truth sits. The usual escapes are to take the least favorable case, where everything binds, which is safe and conservative, or to estimate which inequalities nearly bind and treat those as binding, as generalized moment selection does. Both need the faces. Kitamura and Stoye’s cone is given by its generators, in the form {Aν:ν≥0}, and they note that converting it to faces is out of reach in realistic problems. Their cones have hundreds of thousands of generators. The next two sections are about how they avoid the faces altogether.

10.3Why resampling the data is not enough

The bootstrap has a plausible job. We do not know the distribution of J, but we know that π^∗−π^, the wobble of a resampled vector around the sample, imitates π^−π0, the wobble of the sample around the truth. What it cannot do alone is supply the point to wobble around. Kitamura and Stoye single this out as the main difficulty: the sample π^ is not a usable stand-in for the true π0.

To see why, work in the plane, where one can draw everything. The setting that follows is a toy of ours, not an example from any paper and not the survey problem. The plane is measured in standard errors, so the noise is N(0,I). The cone is a wedge of opening 60∘ with its apex at the origin, the null is that the truth is in the wedge, and the truth we examine is the apex, where the two faces meet. Three candidates for the population the bootstrap should wobble around:

  1. The sample itself, π^.

  2. The restricted estimator η^, the nearest point of C to π^. When π^ is outside this puts the population on a face. When π^ is inside, it is π^.

  3. The tightened estimator η^τ, the nearest point of the tightened cone, which Section 10.4 defines.

Whatever the choice, each resampled vector is a draw of noise added to the chosen population, and each gets the same statistic, its squared distance to the cone, so that the bootstrap statistics have a distribution we can compare with the truth’s.

Choice (A) fails in the most basic way. It never rejects. In our toy it rejected 0 of 3,000 samples when the truth was the apex, 0 of 3,000 on an edge, and also 0 of 3,000 when the truth was two standard errors outside the wedge, where a good test should reject more than half the time. The reason is simple. If the sample is outside the cone by some distance δ, a population centered there is outside the cone by δ as well, and bootstrap draws from it are on average at least as far out as the sample was. The sample’s own J is a typical bootstrap value and never an extreme one. Centering on the data mimics the alternative, and a statistic that measures the distance to the null can never be extreme under the alternative it is imitating.

Choice (B) is the standard fix. Center the bootstrap on a point of the null, the nearest one. This resembles the recentering of the J-statistic in overidentified GMM, as the authors note, and for a cone with one face that matters it works. The experiment of cycle 5 below has one such face. Where it breaks is when the truth is near two faces at once. Figure 10.2 shows the problem at the apex.

The left panel shows the target: with the truth at the apex, 83% of the sampling draws fall outside the wedge, and the 95th percentile of J is 4.56. The middle panel shows one sample that happened to land inside the wedge, a little off both faces. For this sample η^=π^, so the bootstrap population sits in the interior, and the bootstrap draws cross a face only 49% of the time. Their 95th percentile of J∗ is 1.76, well under half the right answer, and at this truth a critical value that small would reject far too often. The restricted estimator told the bootstrap, honestly, that the sample is not on any face. But a sample being inside a face by a fraction of a standard error is evidence that the truth could be on the face. It is no evidence that the truth is not. This sample, with J=0, cannot be rejected at all, but a sample that lands just outside one face is treated the same way: the bootstrap is centered on that face, a little off the other one, and its critical value is again too small.

10.4Recentering twice

Kitamura and Stoye’s remedy is in Section 4.2 of their paper. We paraphrase it in four steps, in the notation of this chapter.

  1. Choose τN that goes to zero while NτN goes to infinity. Tighten the cone to Cτ={Aν: ν≥τNH1}=τNHA1+C, where H is the number of generators and 1 a vector of ones. Every generator must be present in the population with at least a small positive weight, which is the same as moving the apex of the cone inward along the sum of the generators, as in Figure 10.1. Find the tightened restricted estimator η^τ, the point of Cτ nearest to π^.

  2. Form the recentered bootstrap draws π^τ∗(r)=π^∗(r)−π^+η^τ: the wobble of the resample around the sample, added to the tightened estimator.

  3. For each such draw compute the statistic against the tightened cone, J∗(r)=Nmin{∥π^τ∗(r)−η∥2:η∈Cτ}.

  4. Take the 1−α quantile of the J∗(r) as the critical value for J.

The authors call this a double recentering. As they explain it, the bootstrap population η^τ is moved off π^ twice: once onto the null, the standard move of choice (B), and again because the cone itself has been tightened. In the procedure the tightening shows up in steps 2 and 3. The draws are moved onto the tightened estimate, and the bootstrap statistic is measured against the tightened cone rather than the original one. Both moves are needed. If the draws are centered on the tightened estimate and the distance is measured to the untightened cone, the bootstrap population sits inside the original cone with some slack and the draws again do not see the faces, as in the middle panel of Figure 10.2.

Look at the right panel. The tightened wedge is dashed. The sample is below its apex, so the nearest point of the tightened wedge is the tightened apex itself. The bootstrap is therefore wobbling around the apex of the tightened wedge, which is exactly the situation of the left panel with the wedge shifted. The share of draws outside, 83%, and the 95th percentile of J∗, 4.58, match the truth’s 83% and 4.56. In this toy, with the truth at the apex and τN=log⁡500=2.49, the tightened estimator lands on the tightened apex for 94% of samples (our calculation, same script). That is the whole mechanism: when a face is close enough to the truth that the sample cannot tell, tightening moves the estimator onto the face, and the bootstrap then sees the face as the truth does.

What the authors prove, as we read the statement of Theorem 4.2, is that under a diagonal positive weighting, assumptions on the sampling scheme and a regularity condition on the true π that they give in an appendix, the probability of failing to reject tends to 1−α when the truth is the least favorable point of the null, uniformly over the class. So the test controls size uniformly, not just point by point. They explain the diagonal weighting by a geometric property of the generators. Tightening can turn faces with a little slack into binding ones, and it must not turn a binding face into a slack one. They prove this for the identity weighting when all corners of the cone are acute, and say that in their application the cone’s geometry extends it to any diagonal weighting. One more feature is worth knowing for the survey problem. Because the tightening acts on the weights ν, it leaves equality constraints, such as agreeing margins, unchanged. The authors remark that this is what lets the method run when equality constraints are present.

The price of the guarantee is conservatism. In our toy, with N=500, the tightened test rejected 5.4% of samples when the truth was the apex, 2.1% when the truth was on an edge at distance 1 from the apex, and 4.5% at distance 3. The untightened restricted bootstrap, choice (B), rejected 7.2%, 4.2% and 5.8% in the same cells, and, two standard errors outside the wedge, rejected 55.8% where the tightened test rejected 55.4%. Each of those rates comes from 3,000 samples with 200 bootstrap draws each, which puts the Monte Carlo error near 0.4 percentage points around 5%. The untightened bootstrap over-rejects at the apex, where two faces bind, and is close to right on an edge far from the apex; the tightened one holds the level everywhere and pays for it near edges. Power at the outside point is the same. The tuning value we used, τN=log⁡N/N, is our choice of a sequence that meets their two rate conditions, not a value from the paper.

10.5Splitting the statistic

Return to the survey problem and to the fact that matters here: the cone lies inside the seven-dimensional subspace L, and samples do not. Let PL be the orthogonal projection on L. For any η∈C, the vector π^−η is the sum of π^−PLπ^, which is orthogonal to L, and PLπ^−η, which lies in L. Pythagoras gives ∥π^−η∥2=∥π^−PLπ^∥2+∥PLπ^−η∥2, and only the second term depends on η. Minimizing over the cone leaves J=N∥π^−PLπ^∥2⏟Jmargin+Nminη∈C∥PLπ^−η∥2⏟Jloop. Figure 10.3 draws the right triangle. The check in the script for cycle 5 reproduced this identity on random inputs with a largest error of 4×10−17.

The two parts have different characters.

The margin part is the squared length of a vector that is linear in the data and lives in a three-dimensional space, one dimension for each shared margin. It has no cone in it, no kink and no boundary problem. When all margins are one half, its asymptotic distribution is a weighted sum of three independent χ12 variables, and we computed the weights from the multinomial covariance (figures/ch10_check.py). For a=b=0.2 and d=0.2 they are 0.100, 0.325 and 0.325, and for d=0.5 they are 0.144, 0.250 and 0.356. They shift with d, but they always add to 0.75, so the expected margin part is 0.75 whatever the third survey reports. The values that cycle 5’s simulations produced, from 0.71 to 0.77 across eight designs, agree with that.

The loop part is the distance from the pooled report PLπ^ to the cone. Projecting on L first is the least-squares act of reconciling the margins into one common set, which the memo calls pooling. What remains is the question of whether the pooled tables fit together. That is all the cone-testing difficulty of the previous sections, and it is all located in this part.

The split suggests testing Jloop on its own. The margin part carries noise about margins and nothing about the loop, and in the design below its mean does not move with the third survey at all. Adding it to the statistic adds variance without adding signal, which is the dilution of omnibus tests met in Chapter 3. The test on the loop part uses the same recipe as before, with the loop part of the bootstrap statistic computed from the recentered draws. Whether Kitamura and Stoye’s theorem covers a cone that sits in a proper subspace because the surveys share margins we have not checked. We read their procedure and not their proof.

10.6What the papers say

What the paper says: Kitamura and Stoye (2018), “Nonparametric analysis of random utility models”

The paper asks whether repeated cross-sections of demand, on several budget sets, could have been generated by one population of rational consumers, with unrestricted heterogeneity. Their Theorem 3.1 gives a necessary and sufficient condition of the form we have used: the stacked choice probabilities π equal Aν for some ν≥0, where each column of A records the choices of one rational type on every budget. The statistic is the weighted squared distance from the sample to the cone, and the critical value comes from the double recentering of Section 10.4. The theory claims uniform size control over a class of data-generating processes. They motivate the cone in the vertex form by the size of the problem: the largest matrices in their application have 78 or 79 rows and over 300,000 columns, so the facets cannot be computed. Their empirical application, on the U.K. Family Expenditure Survey, finds estimated demands that are not rationalizable, with a rejection that is not statistically significant, as the introduction says. They give a three-budget example with twelve patches, as in our twelve cells, as the smallest case where checking the budgets in pairs tests only a necessary condition, so a loop of the same kind appears there.

What it does not say. A word search of the paper finds no mention of Bell, contextuality or Vorob’ev. In revealed preference the budgets share no observable, so the only equality constraint in the passages we read is that probabilities add up. The overlapping margins of the survey problem, and the equality constraints they create, are our addition. We read the arXiv version’s abstract, Sections 1 to 3.2 and 4.1 to 4.3 in full, plus for this chapter the statements of Lemma 4.1 and Theorem 4.2 with the paragraphs that follow them, and two examples of Section 3.3, the three-budget one and the one that gives the sizes of their matrices; not the proofs, the Monte Carlo, the application or the appendices.

What the paper says: Hoderlein and Stoye (2014), “Revealed preferences in a heterogeneous population”

They work with the same kind of data, repeated cross-sections, and a weaker axiom, the weak axiom of revealed preference. The share of consumers who violate it depends on the joint distribution of choices across two budgets, and repeated cross-sections give only the margins; the part that is not identified is the copula. Their Proposition 1 gives sharp bounds on the share of violators from the Fréchet–Hoeffding limit copulas. With three or more goods, the worst-case copulas lose a plain interpretation, and some natural restrictions create violations that are artifacts. Where Kitamura and Stoye ask whether the set of rationalizing populations is empty, Hoderlein and Stoye ask how large the share of violators can be, from below and from above, which is the pairing of test and uncertainty from Chapter 8.

What we read. Only the authors’ March 2013 version: the introduction up to the opening of the related literature, and Proposition 1 with its discussion. We have not read their inference, which the introduction says applies moment-inequality methods, and our account of the three-good case is from the introduction.

What the paper says: Fang, Santos, Shaikh and Torgovitsky (2023), “Inference for large-scale linear systems with known coefficients”

Their null is the one we have been using: a vector β(P) that can be estimated equals Ax for some x≥0, with a known matrix A. They list Kitamura and Stoye’s random utility test as an example, along with bounds on treatment effects and the feasibility of linear programs with known coefficients. Their Theorem 3.1, as we read it, splits the null into two geometric requirements. First, β must lie in the range of A. Second, the minimum-norm solution x∗ of Ax=β must pass a sign condition, which is another way of saying that adding some vector from the null space of A must make it non-negative. Their test statistic is built from these two requirements: it is the larger of a linear program that detects a β outside the range and one that detects a failing sign condition. They design the test for problems with so many columns that Fourier–Motzkin elimination, a classical way of turning generators into faces, is out of reach.

Our observation, not theirs. In the pooled survey problem, the matrix A is the same 12×8 matrix. That β lies in its range means that the shared margins agree, the margin part of cycle 5’s split. The null space of A is spanned by the alternating vector k of Section 10.1, so the sign requirement says that the minimum-norm weights ν∗ can be shifted by some multiple tk to become non-negative. That is cone membership of the pooled report, the loop part. The range part and the sign part of their statistic are therefore the margin and loop parts of ours. The correspondence is of what each part detects, and not of numbers: theirs are linear programs over norm balls, ours are squared distances, and we have not computed theirs. We read their abstract, introduction and the statement of Theorem 3.1, and later their Section 4.1 with the statistic; their critical value (Section 4.3) and proofs we have not read. Chapter 15 takes up Fang and Santos (2019), on inference when a statistic has a kink.

10.7What we found

Cycle 4 had used a made-up critical value for the survey triangle. The question of cycle 5 was whether Kitamura and Stoye’s test works on overlapping surveys, and whether it matters that the margins of the surveys disagree in sample. The setting was the one of Chapter 8, with a=b=0.2, so that the band for d is [0,0.4], and 400 simulated studies per cell, 200 bootstrap draws each, a five percent level, and τN=log⁡N/N, which was our choice within the paper’s rate conditions. The decomposition was verified to 4×10−17. The experiment script is research/experiments/exp5_cone_test.py, and the results sit in the file beside it.

The Monte Carlo standard error is about 0.011 at a rate of 0.05 and about 0.025 at 0.5. Chapter 8 printed six of these rows, and the other two are here. Three things in the table deserve a sentence each.

On the face, d=0.4, the loop test rejects 4.5 and 4.3 percent, so its size is about right at both sample sizes. In the interior it never rejects, as it should not: Jloop is exactly zero there. The total statistic rejects about 5 percent even deep inside, 4.3 and 5.8 percent, because its margin part is always live. That is not a flaw in the total test, which has the right size, but it spends its level on a part that has nothing to do with the loop.

Just outside the face the loop part has a good deal more power: 0.40 against 0.145 at n=500, and 0.885 against 0.525 at n=2000, for a third survey at d=0.45. At d=0.5 and n=500 the figures are 0.925 against 0.590. Averages show where the difference comes from:

The margin part sits near 0.75 in every design, whatever the third survey says, as Section 10.5 predicts, and it does not see the loop at all. The loop part grows with d and with n. This is the finding of Chapter 3 again: the omnibus statistic dilutes the part that carries the signal.

Three mistakes

The first run rejected 7.5 percent of the time in the interior. Deep inside the tetrahedron the loop part of the statistic and its bootstrap quantile are both zero apart from rounding, and the comparison was between rounding errors. The notebook does not record which column showed the 7.5 percent. Only the loop part can be exactly zero, so we take it to have been there. A tolerance fixed it, and the saved results come from the corrected run. To see the mechanism, we reran a stripped-down version for this chapter (figures/ch10_check.py, 400 samples at d=0.2, n=500, 100 bootstrap draws): with no tolerance, 27 of 400 samples rejected, 6.75 percent, and with the 10−9 tolerance none did. The largest statistic anywhere in that run was about 10−28. Comparing two rounding errors rejects at roughly the nominal five percent by chance, which is no property of the test.

Tightening made no difference in cycle 5. The untightened bootstrap gave identical rates in every cell. The reason is that one inequality binds in this design, and that is the case where choice (B) of Section 10.3 works. The part of Kitamura and Stoye’s method that is hard, several inequalities nearly binding at once, is not exercised by this experiment. Figure 10.2 is the picture of what the experiment did not test. Chapters 15 and 16 take up what happened when the second of the two moves of Section 10.4 was left out in a harder problem, in cycle 15, which resampled from the tightened fit but measured the shortfall against the untightened null, so that it made the first move without the second.

A quotation that would not verify. In cycle 17 we had written a sentence about the second recentering in quotation marks and attributed it to the paper. A verification search did not find it in the stored text, and the memo and reading notes now paraphrase. Reading the stored text afterwards, we find the idea of a second recentering there, and the match may have failed on the mathematical markup around the symbol for the cone. Either way, nothing here is quoted.

Two further limits come with the numbers. The cycle used identity weighting. With unequal sample sizes or non-uniform tables the split needs a weighted projection, and the same weight must be used in both parts, or the Pythagoras step fails. That has not been done. And the design is the smallest possible: two answers per question, three surveys, one face in play. The verdict recorded at the time was that inference for the obstruction on probability tables is available off the shelf, and that for overlapping surveys it should be applied to the loop part after reconciling the margins. There is no new theory in that, and nothing here is established as new. The notebook added that the split is probably contextuality-by-default under another name. Cycle 6 confirmed half of that: the distinction dates from 2015, but the two treat disagreeing margins differently, and Chapter 11 says how.
