Chapter 12

What survey methodologists know

Web edition. Figure 12.4 is live: it carries the chapter’s question-order simulator.

Here is an invented example, small enough to hold in your head. A pollster asks two yes-or-no questions: X, do you trust the city council, and Z, do you trust the mayor. Half the sample hears the council question first and half hears the mayor question first. Each half returns a two-by-two table of shares (Figure 12.1). Every number in the figure is made up.

Compare the tables cell by cell. Fifteen points of the sample moved from “trust neither” to “trust both”. Trust in the council went from 0.50 to 0.65 and trust in the mayor from 0.55 to 0.70. The share of people who gave the same answer to both questions stayed at 0.75. Mass moved along a diagonal of the table and never left it. That pattern has a name, the QQ equality, and Wang, Solloway, Shiffrin and Busemeyer report that it holds across 66 national polls. Section 12.3 says what they did and did not show.

Chapter 11 left a question open. When a question shared by two surveys is answered differently in each, two verdicts about whether the surveys could describe one population are available, pooling and contextuality by default, and each can be wrong in its own way. Which way depends on how the answers changed. Survey methodologists have measured how answers change when the context of a question changes, and they call these changes context effects or order effects. This chapter sorts their findings by one criterion, which cells of the table lose people and which gain them, and then reads the sort against the two verdicts. By the end you should be able to look at a context effect and say which of three kinds it is, what each verdict will do with it, and why a table of differences does not by itself tell you the kind.

12.1A table is a point in a tetrahedron

Code a yes as +1 and a no as −1, and write sX and sZ for the coded answers. Three numbers describe the table: mX=EsX,mZ=EsZ,c=EsXsZ.mX=EsX,mZ=EsZ,c=EsXsZ. The first two are the margins. The third is the product moment, which is a correlation only when the margins are zero, and it is a linear function of agreement: c=2P(same)−1. Nothing else is needed, because the cell for answers (x,z)∈{±1}2 has share 14(1+xmX+zmZ+xzc). A two-by-two table has three free numbers and these are a convenient three.

The set of all tables is then a solid. Its corners are the four pure tables, in which everybody gives the same pair of answers: (mX,mZ,c)=(−1,−1,1) when everybody says no to both, (1,1,1) when everybody says yes to both, and (−1,1,−1) and (1,−1,−1) for the two ways of splitting. Mix those four in any proportions and you reach every table, and only tables. The solid is a regular tetrahedron (Figure 12.2). It has the same shape as the one in Chapter 8 but lives in a different space. That tetrahedron held the reports of three tables at once. This one holds a single table.

Height is agreement, so a horizontal plane is a set of tables with the same agreement. The tetrahedron has two corners at the top, no–no and yes–yes, and two at the bottom, so every horizontal slice is a rectangle, and there are exactly two directions to move in it: along the direction that joins the top corners, and along the one that joins the bottom corners. In the table the first is a transfer between the two cells where the answers agree, and the second a transfer between the two cells where they disagree. That is the QQ equality as a picture: it says the second context’s table lies in the same horizontal slice as the first.

Count what it takes to specify such a difference. Let it move u people from the no–no cell to the yes–yes cell and v from the no–yes cell to the yes–no cell, with X first, so that the changes in the four cells (00,01,10,11) are (−u,−v,v,u). The probability of a yes to X changes by u+v and that of a yes to Z by u−v. Two margin differences and two unknowns, so you can solve for u and v: u=12(ΔPX+ΔPZ),v=12(ΔPX−ΔPZ),u=12(ΔPX+ΔPZ),v=12(ΔPX−ΔPZ), where ΔPX is the change in the probability of a yes to X. In Figure 12.1 both are +0.15, so u=0.15 and v=0. The upshot is that a QQ-obeying difference is fully determined by the two margin differences. A test that compares margins sees all of it.

A difference between two tables has three coordinates, two margin differences and one change in agreement, and the QQ equality says the third is zero. The opposite corner of the space is a difference with the first two zero. Move t from each of the two disagreeing cells to the agreeing ones, (t,−t,−t,t), and no margin changes while agreement changes by 2t. That is the move a margin test cannot see. A general difference does both.

One thing the three coordinates cannot do is tell you the flow. The same difference (t,−t,−t,t) comes from respondents who change their answer to X (01 becomes 11 and 10 becomes 00), or from respondents who change their answer to Z (01 becomes 00 and 10 becomes 11), or from a mixture. The two tables are the same, the mechanisms are not, and both verdicts care, as the next section shows. So the sorting that follows is a sorting of mechanisms, not of differences between tables. The memo makes the same point as a statement about identification: tables do not identify the rule for comparing the two measurements of a shared question.

12.2What the two verdicts make of each kind

Put the effect into the triangle of Chapter 8. A population exists behind three questions X,Y,Z. Survey 1 reports the (X,Y) table and survey 3 the (Y,Z) table faithfully, and survey 2 reports the (X,Z) table with a context effect in it. Because a population exists by construction, any verdict of “these surveys cannot come from one population” is a false alarm. The verdicts are the two of the last chapters.

Pooling averages each shared margin over the two surveys that carry it, keeps the correlations as reported, and asks whether the result lies in the set of tables one population could produce, the cone of Chapter 10. Contextuality by default treats X in survey 1 and X in survey 2 as two variables, asks that they agree as often as their two margins allow (when the margins differ by δ that is at most 1−δ), and calls the system contextual when no joint distribution of the six variables does that and also reproduces the three tables. For three binary variables in a loop it is a closed-form criterion computed from the tables.

The condition under which the second verdict is safe is elementary. Suppose the reported X differs from the true X by moving some respondents from 0 to 1 and some from 1 to 0, in masses a and b. The margins differ by |a−b|, so the copies could agree on 1−|a−b| of the people, but they in fact agree on 1−a−b. The misreport is a maximal coupling exactly when a or b is zero, that is, when no value both gains and loses respondents. If that holds for every shared variable, the true population together with the misreport supplies a joint distribution in which the copies agree as often as the margins allow, and the by-default verdict is “not contextual”. That is a statement about the mechanism. It does not require the tables to look special.

This sorts mechanisms into three kinds, drawn in Figure 12.3.

One-way shift.

One variable changes in one direction only, possibly depending on the other answer. Its margin moves, the agreement may move, and no value both gains and loses. The one-sided mechanism of cycle 8 (Chapter 11) is the example: a respondent whose Z is yes and whose true X is no reports yes with probability s.

Within-diagonal.

Mass moves between the two agreeing cells, or between the two disagreeing cells. Both margins move and agreement does not. The QQ equality says that reversing the order of two adjacent questions has this form.

Two-way effect on the association.

A respondent whose answers disagree is moved to agree (assimilation), or the reverse (contrast). One variable gains and loses at once, so the margin can stay where it was while the correlation moves.

Take them in turn.

One-way. A one-way shift on a yes-or-no variable changes exactly as many answers as its margin shifts, so the by-default verdict is right: no false alarm in the 20,000 populations of cycle 8. Pooling is not: at s=0.4 it raised false alarms in 10.1 percent of diffuse and 56.5 percent of concentrated populations, because it averages a margin that really did move.

Within-diagonal. Take the move of share s of the no–no cell to the yes–yes cell. Respondents in that mass have X changed from no to yes and Z changed from no to yes, and nobody else changes. Each variable moves one way, by exactly the mass that its margin shifts. Both couplings are maximal. Experiment 8b, which Section 12.4 reports, found no by-default false alarm. Pooling again raises false alarms.

Two-way. Here I want the arithmetic, because it is the case that defeats both verdicts. Take a table with both margins at one half. Then r=EsXsZ is the correlation and the share who disagree is δ=(1−r)/2. Assimilation at size s sends the fraction s of each disagreeing cell to the agreeing cell where X matches Z. Disagreement becomes (1−s)δ, the margin of X does not move (each value gains sδ/2 and loses the same), and r′=1−2(1−s)δ=1−(1−s)(1−r). The effect lives in the third coordinate. Yet X changes for sδ of the people and its margin required nothing, so the coupling is not maximal, and the shortfall is the whole sδ. Figure 12.4 shows this for the correlations Schwarz, Strack and Mai report. No margin test has anything to find. Both verdicts raise false alarms, and cycle 8 measured how many (Section 12.4).

12.3What the papers say

We read four sources and one of them only in its abstract. Reading depth matters here because the conclusions lean on how large and how general the evidence is, so each entry says what was read.

What the paper says: Schwarz, Strack and Mai (1991), Assimilation and contrast effects in part-whole question sequences: a conversational logic analysis

Read through the conclusions in a scanned copy with character errors; the numbers quoted below were legible in both text and table. The study surveyed 456 German adults approached in a pedestrian mall, about 50 per condition. It asks a general question (life satisfaction) and a specific one (satisfaction with the relationship), in both orders. The correlation of the two ratings was 0.32 with the general question first, 0.67 with the specific question first and 0.18 with the specific question first behind a lead-in that presented the two questions together. Conditions labeled explicit inclusion and explicit exclusion gave 0.61 and 0.20, and three specific questions asked first gave 0.46. Means moved by the valence of the specific answer: between the two orders, happy respondents’ mean went from 8.5 to 9.5 and unhappy respondents’ from 6.8 to 5.8, the second not significant, as our notes have it. The authors write that differences in correlations, not in means, are the more adequate test. That is the two-way mechanism, on 11-point scales, with a part-whole relation between the questions. What the paper does not give is a large sample: this is a convenience sample in one place, several contrasts are not significant, and nothing in what we read measures how the effect would show up as a difference between two surveys that each carry the question.

What the paper says: Wang, Solloway, Shiffrin and Busemeyer (2014), Context effects produced by question orders reveal quantum nature of human judgments

Main text read in full; the supplement, which holds the proof and the tests, was not read. The authors take 66 Pew surveys from 2001 to 2011, chosen only because each randomized the order of two adjacent yes-or-no questions. Order effects are present overall (their test gives p=0.0004). The QQ equality, that the probability of giving the same answer to both questions is the same in both orders, is not rejected: their statistic q for the departure from it is not distinguishable from zero (p=0.46). The two order effects on a diagonal should sum to zero, and across the studies the paired diagonal effects correlate at −0.82 against a predicted −1. The condition is that the questions are back to back; a poll that inserted information between them violates the equality, with q=0.15. The authors say they know of no classical account that produces both order effects and the equality; two classical models that satisfy it are in the supplement. What the paper does not do is test the equality on rating scales, which is where Schwarz, Strack and Mai found their largest effects. The main text does not list the Pew questions, so we cannot say whether any pair is of the part-whole kind, and the selection of surveys depends on having an order manipulation. The memo records that Dzhafarov, Zhang and Kujala report no contextuality in behavioral data once margins may differ; we read only their abstract and conclusion.

What the paper says: Deaton and Stone (2016), Understanding context effects for a measure of life evaluation: how responses matter

Read in full in the author manuscript. It analyses a randomized trial inside the Gallup daily poll, 76,167 interviews between January and April 2009. Half the sample was asked two political questions, approval of the President and satisfaction with the way things are going in the country, before the Cantril ladder, and half got the ladder first. Asking them first lowered the ladder by 0.67 of a rung, against a mean of 6.45 and a standard deviation of 2.12. The effect comes from the answers: respondents dissatisfied with the country, about 80 percent, report a ladder 0.87 lower, and satisfied respondents show no change. The effect differs by group enough to reverse the ranking of two groups, and a uniform shift would leave test-retest reliability untouched, so reliability does not protect a comparison across contexts. Gallup now asks the political and wellbeing questions of different respondents, which removes the context effect and creates the separate-survey problem of Chapters 8 to 11. The paper measures a shift in a mean on an eleven-point scale. It does not say whether respondents moved by one step or several, or whether any value both gained and lost, so the classification as a one-way shift is a reading of ours and not a result of theirs.

What the paper says: Tourangeau, Singer and Presser (2003), Context effects in attitude surveys: effects on remote items and impact on predictive validity

Abstract only. It reports that four of five order experiments changed the answers to the items directly involved, and that the changes did not carry to later items, to relations with background characteristics or to the relation with a recorded behavior, which is a census return. The authors’ conclusion, as far as the abstract goes, is that such effects are generally local. Without the paper we cannot say how large the effects were, what the questions were, or whether any was of the part-whole kind. A local effect is good news for the bias a context effect puts into later answers. It says nothing on whether the effect changes the association between the two questions asked together, which is what the sorting is about.

Not obtained. Schimmack and Oishi (2005), a meta-analysis of the part-whole effect on such correlations, and the standard books on survey response were not obtained. Our recollection is that the meta-analysis finds the effect small on average. That is recollection and we have not checked it. If it is right, the two-way effect would be less common in practice than Schwarz, Strack and Mai’s single study suggests.

12.4What we found

Cycle 8 had two kinds of mechanism: two-way (assimilation and contrast) and one-way. The reading found both in the survey literature, and a third that cycle 8 did not have, the within-diagonal move. Experiment 8b adds it.

Experiment 8b. Population tables, no sampling. A random population of the eight kinds of people is drawn, either diffuse (shares from a Dirichlet distribution with parameter 1) or concentrated (parameter 0.2, which puts most mass on a few kinds and therefore sits near the edge of the cone). The (X,Z) table is moved: a share s of the no–no cell goes to yes–yes, or a share s of the no–yes cell goes to yes–no. Five thousand populations per cell, two kinds of population, two moves and s equal to 0.1, 0.4 and 0.8 make twelve cells and 60,000 populations. The largest change in agreement the move produced was 2.2×10−16, so the QQ equality holds to rounding by construction. The by-default verdict raised no false alarm in any of the twelve cells. Pooling did (Table 12.1, Figure 12.5).

Pooling raises false alarms at every kind of effect, and by default raises them at the one that changes an association. How large the pooling figure is depends more on the population than on the mechanism: the concentrated populations are near the boundary, where a small distortion can cross it, and the diffuse ones are not. The sorting says which verdict can be trusted. It does not say how often pooling will mislead, which needs the population.

How big is the dangerous one? We converted the correlations in Schwarz, Strack and Mai to an assimilation size. With margins at one half and disagreement (1−r)/2, the move from r=0.32 to r=0.67 takes disagreement from 0.34 to 0.165, which is s=1−0.165/0.34=0.51. The earlier study they cite, with 0.16 and 0.55, gives 0.46. The size 0.4 used in cycle 8 is therefore not extreme for this evidence, and the nearest cell in the table above is s=0.4: false alarms from the by-default verdict in 8.0 percent of diffuse and 25.0 percent of concentrated populations. The joint lead-in goes the other way. From 0.32 to 0.18, disagreement rises from 0.34 to 0.41, which is a contrast of size (0.41−0.34)/(1−0.34)=0.11. These three numbers are our arithmetic on the reported correlations. The conversion treats 11-point ratings as symmetric binary answers and takes the first condition as the baseline, which is a choice and not a fact: no condition is the truth.

Something we checked while writing this. The memo explains the silence of the by-default verdict under the within-diagonal move by saying that each variable changes exactly as much as its margin. That is true for one diagonal at a time, which is all experiment 8b did. Move both diagonals at once and one variable becomes two-way: with 00 to 11 and 01 to 10, X only moves up but Z moves up from the first and down from the second (the combination of 00 to 11 with 10 to 01 makes X the two-way variable). We ran that with the same generators and verdicts (figures/ch12_numbers.py, seed 20261201), in 5,000 random populations for each of two combinations, two kinds of population and s of 0.1, 0.4 and 0.8, which is 60,000 populations. The by-default verdict raised no false alarm. Pooling raised them in 0.4 to 71 percent of populations, by the same pattern as before. So the condition that no value both gains and loses is sufficient, as the argument shows, and it is not necessary for silence, which the simulation shows. The first section says why. The verdict sees only tables, and a table can come from more than one flow. With masses a moved from 00 to 11 and b from 01 to 10, and a≥b, moving b from 01 to 11, b from 00 to 10 and a−b from 00 to 11 gives the same table, and in that flow X and Z each move only up. (When a<b, move a from 01 to 11, b−a from 01 to 10 and a from 00 to 10; Z then moves only down.) The sufficient condition holds for that flow, so the true population with that flow supplies the joint distribution the verdict asks for. A construction of the same kind works for any change that keeps agreement fixed, once each question is turned so that its yes share does not fall. It is also a population-table result, with binary variables and no sampling.

Mistakes, and limits. Three should be said plainly.

  • The memo recorded that cycle 7’s first grid showed pooling never raising a false alarm, and that a wider search with random populations found that it does. The same lesson applies here: a structured example can hide a verdict’s weakness that random populations show. The diffuse column of Table 12.1 alone would suggest pooling is nearly harmless under within-diagonal moves, and the concentrated column says it is not.

  • The reading was thin. Two sources were read in full (Wang et al. without its supplement, which holds the proof), one through the conclusions of a scan with errors, and one as an abstract. The Tourangeau paper is cited above for what its abstract says and not for anything else.

  • The mapping from the literature to the experiment is ours. These studies are about the order of questions inside one survey. The experiment puts a context effect between two surveys that carry the same question with different companions. Whether an order effect in one survey transfers to a difference between surveys is an assumption, not a finding.

Where this leaves the sorting. The large-sample evidence we read, the Gallup trial and the Pew polls, is of the kinds that move margins: a shift in a mean, and mass moving within a diagonal. These are the effects a margin test detects and the by-default verdict handles. The dangerous kind, a context effect on associations that leaves margins alone, rests in what we read on small experiments with rating scales and a general question after a specific one. The QQ equality, which holds agreement fixed, and the part-whole results, in which the correlation roughly doubles, pull against each other, and we did not find a study that tests both on one data set. They concern different kinds of questions, so there may be no conflict. Nothing here establishes a new result. The sorting is a way of reading known results against two verdicts, and the memo’s identification point stands: a difference in how a shared question is measured and a failure of the surveys to cohere cannot be told apart without a rule for comparing the two measurements.
