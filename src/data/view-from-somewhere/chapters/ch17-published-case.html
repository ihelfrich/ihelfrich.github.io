A published case

Web edition. Figure 17.5 is live: you can resimulate its lines. A panel after Figure 17.3 works out dominance and forced flows for any two sets of counts.

Picture one trial. A volunteer’s head is steadied in a chin-rest ninety centimeters from a monitor. On a black screen two gray circles, their centers 320 pixels apart, stay in place for the whole session. Inside each circle sits a dot four pixels across, either exactly in the middle or moved four pixels to the left, to the right, up or down. A shift that small is about four minutes of arc, and it is hard to see. The volunteer presses two keys together, one for where the dot was in the left circle and one for the right circle, and the dots vanish. Six hundred milliseconds later the next pair appears.

Three people did this, for twenty to twenty-three half-hour sessions each, about 380 trials a session. Cervantes and Dzhafarov (2019) took the responses and reported contextuality in the behavior of single individuals. Earlier demonstrations of contextuality in behavior had pooled the answers of many people, each of whom answered in a single context. The claim was published in the Journal of Mathematical Psychology by authors of the theory of contextuality by default, and it is the one place in this book where we can hold someone else’s finished empirical claim against everything the earlier chapters built.

So this chapter does what the earlier ones did with theory: it takes the claim apart and puts it back together. By the end you will know what the verdict is a statement about, which part of it we reproduced from the published tables, which part of the paper’s evidence of reliability we could not keep, and what is still open. The short version is that the point estimates do give the verdict, a later rule about which questions may be asked of the data leaves it standing, and the bootstrap shares that were meant to show it is reliable would look the same if nothing were there. Whether anything is there, a valid test has not told us.

17.1Twenty-five arrangements of two dots

Each circle’s dot has five positions and each response has five options: center, left, right, up, down. Pair a position for the left dot with one for the right dot and you get twenty-five arrangements. In the vocabulary of Chapter 11, each arrangement is a context. What is measured is a content: the response to the left circle when its dot is at position x is the content the paper writes x−, for example l⁠− when the left dot is at the left; for the right circle it writes −x. There are five contents of each kind, ten in all. A content is measured in five contexts, the five arrangements that fix the dot in that circle and let the other one roam (Figure 17.1).

Nobody intended the right dot to touch the response to the left one. If it does, the response distribution for a content shifts from context to context while the stimulus the content names stays put. Chapter 11 called that inconsistent connectedness, and it is the situation the whole of contextuality by default was built for.

The paper’s design target was at least about 300 trials in every context, chosen so that confidence intervals for proportions cover well, and the appendix tables of the paper list 333 to 337 trials for every context of every participant (our reading of the tables, in ch17_collapse.py). The analysis uses the two five-way response distributions of each context, the left response and the right response, and nothing else. It does not use how the two responses of one trial go together. That is worth saying plainly, because the word contextuality tends to suggest the within-context joint is what fails to glue. Here it is not touched. The verdict concerns how a content’s response distribution shifts across contexts. In the language of Part III it is a verdict about margins.

17.2When two distributions can follow each other

Take one content and two of its contexts, with response distributions p=(p1,…,p5) in one and q in the other. We never see the response in both contexts at once, so a joint distribution of the pair is something we choose. It is a coupling, a table rij=P(first=i, second=j) with row sums p and column sums q. Contextuality by default asks for the coupling in which the two copies agree as much as their margins allow. With five values, “agree” can be asked at several levels of coarseness, so the theory asks it of every yes-or-no question about the response: was it left? was it left or up? was it center, left or right? Each subset of positions and its complement define one of these splits. Five positions give fifteen of them, five that single out one position and ten that single out two, since a three-position split is the two-position split of the rest.

For the one-position splits, agreeing as much as the margins allow means rii=min(pi,qi)for every i,rii=min(pi,qi)for every i, and for the split {i,j} it means rii+rij+rji+rjj=min(pi+pj, qi+qj). These are the conditions in Section 4 of Dzhafarov, Cervantes and Kujala (2017), as we wrote them down when we read it. What follows from them is our own working, but it is short and you can check it.

Sort the five values into three kinds: surplus values k where pk>qk, with excess ek=pk−qk; deficit values i where pi<qi, with shortfall di=qi−pi; and ties. Because ∑p=∑q=1, the total excess equals the total shortfall. The one-position conditions put min(pi,qi) on the diagonal, so a deficit value i has its whole row pi on the diagonal and nothing off it, and a surplus value k has its whole column qk on the diagonal. Now apply the pair condition to a surplus k and a deficit i: pi+qk+rik+rki=min(pi+pk,qi+qk)=pi+qk+min(ek,di), and rik=0 because row i is used up. So the flow from surplus value k to deficit value i is forced: rki=min(ek,di). The pair condition on two surplus values, or two deficit values, forces their off-diagonal cells to zero in the same way. Row k must send out exactly its excess, so the coupling exists only if ∑i deficitmin(ek,di)=ek  for every surplus k,∑k surplusmin(ek,di)=di  for every deficit i.∑i deficitmin(ek,di)=ek  for every surplus k,∑k surplusmin(ek,di)=di  for every deficit i. Figure 17.2 shows two invented pairs of distributions, and Figure 17.3 shows the couplings the conditions force on them.

If a content has a single deficit value i, then di is the total excess, at least as large as any one ek, so every row sends exactly its excess and the column receives exactly di. The coupling exists. A single surplus value works the same way from the other side. This is nominal dominance: one of the two variables falls short of the other at no more than one value. If there are two or more of each, it breaks. Row k must send ∑imin(ek,di) and the sum has to equal ek. Every term is positive and at most ek. If one term equals ek the others push the sum over it, so every di is below ek and the sum is the total shortfall, which is the total excess. But with a second surplus value l the total excess exceeds ek. Contradiction. So the coupling that makes every split agree as much as it can exists exactly when one distribution nominally dominates the other, which is Corollary 4.5 of the 2017 paper. Two checks of ours back this up: in experiments 20 and 20b the program that asks for maximal agreement on all fifteen splits and the dominance rule gave the same verdict on all 300 pairs of the published data, and in 20,000 random pairs of counts the flow rule above agreed with the sign rule every time (ch17_flows.py).

Dominance is a stringent condition, and the authors of that paper say so. With three values it always holds, since the three differences sum to zero and two negative ones leave a single positive one. With more values it thins out quickly. For two distributions drawn independently and uniformly from the simplex, the chance that one dominates the other is, in our simulation:

For three or more contexts of one content, dominance in every pair is still only necessary: Kujala and Dzhafarov (2022), their Example 20, give three distributions, each pair of which satisfies dominance, that still admit no coupling agreeing maximally everywhere. The paper’s verdict does not need the converse, because one failing pair is enough to call a content contextual.

17.3Which questions may be asked

All fifteen splits are a choice, and they are not the only one. Kujala and Dzhafarov (2022) argue that a split should only be allowed if both of its parts are connected in a sense given by a structure placed on the values. The values come with vicinities: sets of values close enough to each other to count as connected. For these five positions they take a position together with one of its one-step neighbors as a vicinity. The paper spells this out for “left”, whose vicinities pair it with center, up and down, and we have drawn the other three positions the same way (Figure 17.4). Center is next to all four; the four directions form a ring, each next to the two between which it sits. Left and right are not next to each other, nor are up and down.

Count the allowed splits. Of the ten two-position splits, the eight whose pair is joined in the graph (four with center, four on the ring) are allowed, and the two whose pair is not, left with right and up with down, are not. The five one-position splits are all allowed. That gives thirteen of fifteen, which is the number Kujala and Dzhafarov (2022) report for this example.

The choice of allowed splits is not a detail. If the five positions were laid on a line and only cuts were allowed, a single content in several contexts could never be contextual by itself (their Theorem 16, for ordered values). Declaring the answers unordered, with all splits, or ordered, with cuts only, is itself a rule for comparing distributions, a point Chapter 13 develops. Whether the paper’s verdict depends on the two excluded splits is an empirical question, and a cheap one, because the program can be rerun with thirteen splits instead of fifteen. We did that, and the answer is in the last section.

17.4What the paper reports

For each participant the paper tabulates the two response distributions in each of the 25 contexts, finds pairs of contexts sharing a content, and checks nominal dominance for each. Its statement, in Section 2.3, is that for every participant dominance fails for at least one pair in every content. By the 2017 theorem that makes the all-splits representation of every content contextual, so all three participants are contextual.

To say how reliable that is, the authors resample. They draw 100,000 bootstrap resamples per participant, each time resampling with replacement the responses in every context at its own sample size, and record the share of resamples in which nominal dominance holds in every pair of contexts of a content (Table 17.1), and in every pair of every content at the same time. That last number is zero for all three participants. They treat 0 successes in 100,000 trials as binomial data, find the exact 99.999 percent Clopper–Pearson interval [0,0.00012] for the probability of that event, and conclude that the data cannot be random perturbations of a pattern that satisfies dominance. The interval is correct arithmetic: 1−(5×10−6)1/100000 is 1.2×10−4. The question is what the probability being bounded is.

17.5What a zero share can mean

Build the world in which the paper’s null is true, in its simplest form: all five contexts of a content have the same response distribution. Then dominance holds, with ties, and as far as this criterion goes the content is not contextual. Draw one sample of 335 trials from each context. How often does a pair of those samples fail dominance?

Sampling noise moves each of the five proportions up or down, and the pair fails when two proportions of one fall below the other’s while two more rise above. For two equal distributions that happens often. In 200,000 simulated pairs at 335 trials, a pair of identical distributions fails dominance in 86 percent of samples when the five responses are equally likely, in 82 percent for the profile (0.6,0.1,0.1,0.1,0.1) and in 71 percent for (0.9,0.04,0.03,0.02,0.01) (ch17_collapse.py; these are invented profiles, but the appendix tables have median peak proportions from 0.58 to 0.72 for the three participants, in the range of the 0.6 profile). More data do not repair this. The signs of the five differences come from noise that shrinks like 1/n in every coordinate at once, so the pattern of signs does not settle as the trials grow. If anything the failures become more common, because exact ties, which help dominance, grow rarer: at 3,000 trials the three rates are 89, 86 and 80 percent. And a content has ten pairs. When all ten must pass, the sample itself fails somewhere in 100 percent of simulated contents (the memo’s experiment 19c).

Now the bootstrap. It resamples around the sample proportions as if they were the truth, and asks how often all ten pairs line up. When the sample is already in the failing region by a margin as large as the resampling noise, and with ten pairs it nearly always is, resamples rarely line up. The share is small for the reason that fresh samples from the null fail: the dominance region is a thin set of cones, and five noisy points have to fall in it together. I am explaining here, not proving; the simulation is what carries the claim. Figure 17.5 shows it. With five contexts a per-content share of at most 1 in 2,000, the paper’s “zero”, is what the null produces in nearly every content.

The surface of Figure 17.6 is the point of the chapter. Over the trials axis it does not fall, and creeps up as exact ties grow rarer, which is the geometry above in a picture, and over the contexts axis it climbs from nothing at two contexts to nearly all contents at five. A reliability summary that would read the same under the null at every sample size cannot carry a claim about the alternative. It is the problem of Chapter 15 in a different setting: a quantity computed at a point on the boundary of the null, where it does not settle as the sample grows, and a resampling scheme that treats the noisy estimate as the truth.

17.6What the papers say

What the paper says: Cervantes and Dzhafarov (2019), “True contextuality in a psychophysical experiment”

The paper reports the design above, derives contextuality from nominal dominance, tabulates the 25 contexts for each participant and reports the bootstrap shares. We read the abstract, Sections 1.3 and 2.3 and the appendix tables, and the experimental method in part; we did not download the data (doi:10.7910/DVN/FCT9VO). The paper does not say that dominance failure is necessary for contextuality, and it notes that the theorem gives a sufficient condition only. It does not test the null that dominance holds at its boundary, and it does not use the within-context joints. The bootstrap summary is described as establishing reliability; our simulations say that it cannot.

What the paper says: Dzhafarov, Cervantes and Kujala (2017), “Contextuality in canonical systems of random variables”

This is the source of the split representation and of nominal dominance. Sections 1 to 5 were read in full, the supplement with its proofs not. For two copies of one many-valued variable, the representation with all splits is noncontextual exactly when one distribution nominally dominates the other, and the authors call this a stringent necessary condition that is likely to be violated in many empirical systems. The paper leaves open which splits to use for ordered or structured value sets.

What the paper says: Kujala and Dzhafarov (2022), “Contextuality and dichotomizations of random variables”

This is the later work on restricted splits. Sections 1 and 4 to 8 were read in full, Sections 2 and 3 not. It admits a dichotomization only when both its parts are linked in a pretopology of the values, which gives cuts for ordered values, all splits for unordered categories and thirteen of fifteen for the five dot positions. It also contains Theorem 16, on cuts, and Example 20, on three distributions that dominate pairwise and still admit no coupling that agrees maximally on every split. It does not ask how any of this behaves in finite samples, and the pretopology is something the analyst supplies.

What the paper says: Fang and Santos (2019), “Inference on directionally differentiable functions”

We read the abstract and Sections 3.2 and 3.3. Their Theorem 3.1 says that when a map is only directionally differentiable at the parameter and the limit of the estimator is Gaussian, the standard bootstrap is consistent only if the map is fully differentiable there. We take the diagnosis from them, not a theorem: the indicator that a sample satisfies dominance is a step function, and we have not checked whether any of their conditions apply to it. What matters for this chapter is the pattern, that a quantity computed at a boundary point has a sampling distribution the bootstrap does not reproduce.

17.7What we found

This part of the research was cycle 17, on the evening of November 12, 2026: the reading of Cervantes and Dzhafarov, and experiments 19, 19b and 20. The review that followed it led to experiments 20b and 19c. Two things we wrote down that evening had to be corrected, and the order in which they went wrong is the useful part.

First pass: two contexts per content

Experiment 19 simulated the paper’s bootstrap under an exact null with ten contents, two contexts each, 300 trials per context, 2,000 resamples and 1,000 simulated experiments, with the response distribution (0.6,0.1,0.1,0.1,0.1) and with the uniform one. The share of resamples in which dominance held for all contents at once was at most 1 in 2,000 in every one of the 1,000 experiments, so the paper’s overall zero would read the same under the null. But no single content’s share fell that low in any experiment. We concluded that the overall share was uninformative and the per-content shares were not. Experiment 19b pushed on this at the edge of dominance, with two distributions that dominate with ties: three ties, and one tie. In 500 simulated contents each, the sample violated dominance in 78.8 and 67.6 percent of contents; a per-content share of at most 1 in 2,000 occurred in none, and one below 0.01 in 0.4 percent, with a median share of 0.19 and 0.23. Our conclusion was that per-content zeros remain strong evidence even at the edge. That went into the memo.

The mistake is in the design, not the arithmetic. Those experiments had two contexts per content and so one pair. The paper has five contexts and ten pairs, and all ten must dominate.

Second pass: the verdict, and its first set of numbers

Experiment 20 took the published point estimates from the three appendix tables and, for the 100 pairs of contexts sharing a content per participant, asked whether dominance holds and whether a coupling with maximal agreement on all fifteen splits exists, and then on the thirteen allowed splits. The first run disagreed with the theorem on seven pairs. Our dominance check had used the rounded rows as printed while the program normalized them; with both normalized they agreed on all 300 pairs. That run also failed to save its results, because a numpy integer is not valid JSON. The numbers it gave were 41, 68 and 50 failing pairs for the three participants, and 41, 66 and 50 under the thirteen-split rule.

An independent review of cycle 17, 51 findings of which 21 were not confirmed, recomputed this from exact counts. Each row of the appendix tables has a trial count and three-decimal proportions, so the counts k=round(p×trials) can be reconstructed; in experiment 20b all 150 response distributions, two in each of the 75 rows, reproduce their trial counts, and dominance is decided by cross-multiplication of integers, kanb against kbna. The correct numbers are:

P1 47 47 47 10 of 10
P2 64 64 62 10 of 10

Of 100 pairs per participant, dominance fails in 47, 64 and 53. The program with fifteen splits and the dominance rule agree on all 300. Dropping the two splits that the 2022 vicinity structure excludes changes only P2, by two pairs, and every content stays contextual on the point estimates for every participant. So the verdict is a property of the estimates that does not depend on those two splits. The earlier 41, 68 and 50 were an artifact of renormalizing rounded proportions, which turns exact ties into tiny differences. The file exp20_psychophysics_splits_results.json still holds those old numbers, and the corrected ones are in exp20b_exact_counts_results.json.

Third pass: five contexts per content

Experiment 19c repeats the simulation with the paper’s design: five contexts, identical distributions in each (so dominance holds exactly), 335 trials per context, 2,000 resamples and 300 simulated contents per profile. For the profile (0.6,0.1,0.1,0.1,0.1) the share of resamples with dominance in all ten pairs was at most 1 in 2,000 in 100 percent of contents, and for (0.9,0.04,0.03,0.02,0.01) in 98 percent; below 0.01 in 100 percent for both. Every simulated sample violated dominance in some pair. The paper’s per-content zeros are what the null produces, and neither of its bootstrap summaries is evidence. We withdrew the claim of the first pass. The reviewer had made the same point before we reran it, and the lab notebook records it in that order.

Figures 17.5 and 17.6 are our own rerun of the same design over two to five contexts and several sample sizes; the small difference at five contexts (100 and 99 percent in our 200 contents against 100 and 98 percent in the memo’s 300) is simulation noise.

Half of Table 17.1 is not zero, and we did not simulate bootstrap shares for those contents. Experiments 19 to 19c each used two profiles, none with an empty response category, and in the data some contents have empty cells in the pooled distribution (6 of the 50 pooled cells for P1, none for P2, 3 for P3, in the calibration below), which makes dominance easier to satisfy. What the zeros of Table 17.1 cannot do is separate a contextual content from a noncontextual one with five contexts. What the nonzero entries show is not analyzed in the memo.

What is still open

The reviewer’s further claim is that a union test valid at the boundary of dominance, applied to the reconstructed counts, finds no pair with a significant violation. We have not checked that. Such a test would treat a pair as a null made of ten cones, one for each choice of direction and of the single value where dominance may fail, in the manner of the cone tests of Chapters 10 and 16, and would then have to account for there being 100 pairs per participant. Whether it finds contextuality in these data is open, and we do not know which way it will come out.

A calibration written for this chapter

This one is not in the memo. The point-estimate verdict counts failing pairs, and the counts can be set against what noise alone would give. For each content we pooled its five contexts’ reconstructed counts into one distribution, simulated five fresh samples at the actual trial counts from that single distribution, counted failing pairs, and did it 2,000 times (ch17_nullcalib.py). The expected numbers of failing pairs out of 100 are 47.0, 71.7 and 53.1 for the three participants, with central 95 percent intervals of 36 to 57, 62 to 81 and 43 to 64, against the observed 47, 64 and 53 (Figure 17.7). All three observed counts lie inside, P2’s near the lower end. Per content, the observed count of failing pairs lies in the central 90 percent of the simulated counts for 29 of the 30 contents.

What this does and does not say. It says that the headline counts of failing pairs, taken alone, are about what identical distributions would produce, so they cannot be read as a measure of how contextual the data are. It does not say the distributions are identical. The null for the paper’s criterion is a set, every content with all pairs satisfying dominance, and identical distributions are one point of it; at other points, with ties in other places, the failing counts differ, and this is not a valid test of anything. A real shift in a few contexts would also leave the counts near these numbers if it is small relative to the noise. The calibration is a reason not to lean on the counts, not a finding about the data.
