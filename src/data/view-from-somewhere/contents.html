A note before we start

This book has a different job from its predecessor. Nobody Gets the Hill, an earlier and shorter text, taught the idea: local views, the way they are stitched together, and the reason a loop is where the stitching can fail. It stopped at the door of the research. Since then we went through that door seventeen times. We read papers on price indexes, on statistical matching, on databases and Bell’s theorem, on revealed preference, on contextuality in psychology, on survey question order and on inference when a statistic has a kink, and we ran a simulation after every reading to see whether the idea survived.

This book walks through all of it in order. Each chapter has three parts. First the idea, built from the ground with pictures you can turn around in your head. Then what each paper says, in plain words, including what it does not say. Then what we found, with the numbers, the mistakes we made and the corrections, because the corrections taught as much as the results. Most chapters come with a demo: a page you open in a browser and push on until the picture in the chapter becomes obvious.

Two promises carry over from the earlier notes. Every number here was computed before it was typed, by scripts you can rerun. And where the book is unsure, it says so, in the place where the doubt matters rather than in a paragraph of caveats at the end.

Map of the book

Part I. The idea

  1. Local views. Sections, gluing, and why a loop is where gluing can fail.

  2. The bookkeeping machine. Incidence matrices, cycles and cuts.

  3. Gauss’s loops. Least squares on a network and the loop test (research cycle 1).

  4. Frames that do not compose. Gluing with translation, and the piano.

Part II. Price levels across countries

  1. Index numbers and circularity. Fisher, Törnqvist, GEKS.

  2. Tastes that move. Sato–Vartia, Redding and Weinstein, Abe and Rao (cycle 2).

  3. Countries that buy different goods. Feenstra’s variety adjustment (cycle 3).

Part III. Surveys that overlap

  1. Three surveys and a triangle. Statistical matching, the triangle inequalities, and noise (cycles 4 and 5).

  2. Databases and Bell’s theorem. Abramsky’s universal relation and Vorob’ev’s theorem.

  3. Testing a cone. Kitamura and Stoye’s test, and the margin and loop parts of its statistic (cycle 5).

  4. Contextuality by default. When the shared question is answered differently in each survey (cycles 6 to 8).

  5. What survey methodologists know. Context and question-order effects.

  6. More than two answers. Splits, nominal dominance and cuts (cycles 9 to 11).

  7. The bracket. How much a margin gap can hide (cycle 12).

Part IV. Inference at the edge

  1. A statistic with a kink. Why the simple rule fails on the boundary (cycles 13 to 17; Fang and Santos).

  2. Three repairs and their price. The union test, the untightened bootstrap and the derivative bootstrap.

  3. A published case. True contextuality in a psychophysical experiment, re-examined.
